LiveMathematicianBench: A Live Benchmark for Research-Level Mathematical Reasoning with Proof Sketches

Linyang He, Qiyao Yu, Hanze Dong, Baohao Liao, Xinxing Xu, Micah Goldblum et al.
The Brief

This research introduces a new way to test how well advanced AI models, called large language models (LLMs), can do complex math reasoning, like proving new theorems.

The core idea is to create a challenging test using math problems from very recent scientific papers that the AI hasn't seen before, preventing it from just memorizing answers. They also developed tricky wrong answers based on common mistakes in mathematical proofs to really check if the AI understands the logic, not just recognizes patterns. A special test also makes sure the AI isn't just finding the right answer by swapping in parts of the question.

This matters because it helps us understand how close AI is to truly thinking like a mathematician, which could impact how we use AI in scientific discovery and problem-solving in the future.

Background

The field of Artificial Intelligence (AI) is rapidly advancing, with large language models (LLMs) like ChatGPT showing impressive capabilities in understanding and generating human-like text. A key question is whether these models can truly *reason* mathematically, not just regurgitate information. This is crucial because LLMs are increasingly being used in scientific research. However, existing tests for mathematical reasoning in AI often use simplified, artificial problems or rely on data that might have accidentally leaked into the AI's training. This means we can't be sure if the AI is genuinely solving problems or just remembering answers. To address this, researchers need a way to test LLMs on real, cutting-edge mathematical problems that are unlikely to have been in their training data.

What they did

To create a more realistic test for advanced mathematical reasoning, this work introduces

LiveMathematicianBench

. This is a dynamic benchmark, meaning it's designed to be updated with new information. The core idea is to take mathematical theorems recently published on arXiv (a preprint server for scientific papers) *after* the AI models were trained. This "post-cutoff" approach ensures the AI hasn't seen these specific problems before, preventing "data contamination."

The benchmark takes these new theorems and turns them into multiple-choice questions. To make these questions challenging and insightful, several novel techniques were employed:

*

Logical Taxonomy

Theorems are categorized based on their logical structure (e.g., implication, equivalence, existence, uniqueness). This allows for a more nuanced understanding of an AI's strengths and weaknesses across different types of reasoning. *

Proof Sketches

For each theorem, a high-level outline of its proof, called a "proof sketch," is generated. This sketch highlights the main strategic ideas without getting bogged down in technical details. *

Adversarial Distractor Generation

The proof sketches are used to create plausible but incorrect answer choices (distractors). These distractors are designed to mimic common mistakes or misleading lines of reasoning that a mathematician might follow, making it harder for an AI to guess or rely on superficial similarities. *

Substitution-Resistant Mechanism

To prevent AIs from simply plugging in answer choices and checking for consistency, some questions are modified. The correct answer might be replaced with a meta-option like "One of the remaining options is correct, but a stronger result can be proven." This forces the AI to not only identify a correct statement but also to reason about the relative strength of different mathematical claims.

The entire process involves a pipeline that extracts theorems from LaTeX documents, summarizes proofs, classifies theorems, generates questions with carefully crafted distractors, and filters out easy or trivial questions.

What they found

The results show that even the most advanced LLMs struggle significantly with research-level mathematical reasoning.

* In a standard multiple-choice setting, the best model, Gemini-3.1-pro-preview, achieved only

43.5%

accuracy. This indicates that current models are far from mastering this type of reasoning. * When the "substitution-resistant" mechanism was applied, accuracy dropped dramatically. GPT-5.4 scored highest at

30.6%

, while Gemini-3.1-pro-preview fell to

17.6%

, which is below the

20%

chance baseline for guessing. This highlights how much current models rely on shortcut strategies. * Crucially, providing the proof sketches as hints significantly improved performance. Gemini-3.1-pro-preview's accuracy increased by

13.6 percentage points

when it had access to proof sketches, suggesting that LLMs can leverage high-level strategic guidance. * Analysis by logical category revealed that models have different strengths. For example, Gemini-3.1-pro-preview excelled at equivalence and classification problems, while GPT-5.4 variants were better at implication and inequality problems. This shows that mathematical reasoning is not a single, monolithic skill.

Why it matters

LiveMathematicianBench represents a significant step forward in evaluating AI's mathematical reasoning capabilities. By using recent, post-training data and employing sophisticated methods to create challenging questions and distractors, it provides a more realistic and robust testbed than previous benchmarks. The low scores achieved by even the top models underscore that true mathematical reasoning, akin to human research, remains a distant goal for AI.

The findings have several implications:

*

Contamination Resistance

The benchmark's design makes it much harder for models to "cheat" by memorizing answers, offering a more reliable measure of progress. *

Diagnostic Power

The logical taxonomy allows researchers to pinpoint specific areas where LLMs struggle, guiding future development. *

Proof Strategy Integration

The success of proof sketches suggests that AI could potentially benefit from human-like strategic guidance in complex reasoning tasks. This opens avenues for developing AI assistants that can help researchers by suggesting proof strategies. *

Future of AI in Science

As LLMs become more integrated into scientific workflows, benchmarks like this are essential for understanding their limitations and ensuring their responsible and effective use. The takeaway for a non-specialist is that while AI is impressive, it's still a long way from replicating the deep, nuanced reasoning of human mathematicians.
