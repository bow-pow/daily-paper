UNCOVER: The growth of the first massive black holes from JWST/NIRSpec -- spectroscopic redshift confirmation of an X-ray luminous AGN at z=10.1

Andy D. Goulding, Jenny E. Greene, David J. Setton, Ivo Labbe, Rachel Bezanson, Tim B. Miller et al.
The Brief

This research confirms the existence of a massive black hole in the very early universe, observed just a few hundred million years after the Big Bang.

Using the powerful James Webb Space Telescope, scientists spectroscopically verified that a bright X-ray source, named UHZ-1, is indeed a supermassive black hole at a redshift of $z=10.073$. Redshift is a measure of how much light from an object has been stretched by the expansion of the universe, with higher redshifts indicating greater distances and earlier times. The observations suggest this black hole is heavily obscured by gas and dust, hiding its intense activity from optical view, and its host galaxy is relatively small.

This discovery is exciting because it provides strong evidence supporting theories that supermassive black holes might have formed from "heavy seeds" – pre-existing massive black holes – rather than growing from smaller ones, which would have been too slow to reach such sizes so early in cosmic history.

Background

For decades, astronomers have been fascinated by the supermassive black holes (SMBHs) residing at the centers of most galaxies, including our own Milky Way. These colossal objects, millions to billions of times the mass of our Sun, are thought to have grown over cosmic time. A major puzzle is how the very first SMBHs formed so early in the universe's history, when there was relatively little time for them to grow from smaller "seeds." Did they start as small black holes (a few hundred solar masses) and grow incredibly fast, or did they begin as much larger "heavy seeds" (tens of thousands of solar masses) that had a head start? This research aims to shed light on this question by studying one of the most distant and active black holes ever detected.

What they did

The researchers used the James Webb Space Telescope's Near-Infrared Spectrograph (NIRSpec) to observe an object called UHZ-1. This instrument acts like a cosmic prism, splitting light into its constituent colors (wavelengths). By analyzing the specific colors of light emitted by UHZ-1, astronomers can determine its chemical composition, temperature, and, crucially, its distance from us. The farther away an object is, the more the light from it is stretched to longer, redder wavelengths due to the expansion of the universe – a phenomenon called redshift. The team precisely measured the redshift of UHZ-1, confirming it to be at a redshift of $z = 10.073 \pm 0.002$. This means we are seeing UHZ-1 as it was about 13 billion years ago, very early in the universe's history. They also analyzed the light to understand the properties of the galaxy hosting the black hole and compared the black hole's estimated mass to the galaxy's stellar mass.

What they found

The spectroscopic data from NIRSpec confirmed UHZ-1's extreme distance, placing it at a redshift of $z = 10.073 \pm 0.002$. The light from UHZ-1 shows features typical of star formation in galaxies at this early epoch, but surprisingly, it lacks the strong emission lines that usually signal an active, growing black hole (an Active Galactic Nucleus or AGN) in the optical and ultraviolet parts of the spectrum. This lack of visible AGN signatures, combined with a strong X-ray detection from previous observations, suggests that the black hole is heavily obscured, possibly by a thick cloud of gas and dust, making it "Compton-thick." The analysis also revealed that the host galaxy has a stellar mass of about $1.4 \times 10^8$ solar masses. When compared to the estimated black hole mass (around $10^7$ to $10^8$ solar masses), the ratio of black hole mass to stellar mass is significantly higher – two to three orders of magnitude higher – than what is typically observed in galaxies today.

Why it matters

This discovery is significant because it provides strong evidence supporting the "heavy seed" model for the formation of the first supermassive black holes. The extremely high ratio of black hole mass to galaxy mass observed in UHZ-1 at such an early cosmic time is difficult to explain if the black hole started as a small seed. Such a high ratio is more consistent with a scenario where the initial black hole seed was already massive, potentially formed from the direct collapse of a large gas cloud. While the black hole itself is hidden from optical view, its presence is undeniable through its X-ray emission. The fact that the host galaxy's stellar mass is relatively modest further emphasizes the unusual proportion of the black hole. This finding helps us understand the rapid growth of black holes in the early universe, a crucial piece of the puzzle in understanding galaxy evolution. The research also highlights the power of JWST to probe these extremely distant objects and the need for multi-wavelength observations (combining optical, X-ray, and potentially infrared data) to fully characterize these hidden cosmic giants.
