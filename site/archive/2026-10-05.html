Cavity enhanced emission from a silicon T center

Fariba Islam, Chang-Min Lee, Samuel Harper, Mohammad Habibur Rahaman, Yuqi Zhao, Neelesh Kumar Vij et al.
The Brief

This research demonstrates a way to make tiny light-emitting defects in silicon much brighter.

The key idea is to trap the light emitted by these "T centers" (which are like tiny imperfections in the silicon crystal that can emit light when excited) inside a tiny, specially designed optical structure called a "nanophotonic cavity." This cavity acts like a mirror, bouncing the light back and forth and encouraging it to be emitted more strongly at a specific color (the "zero-phonon line"). This dramatically increases how much light is produced and how efficiently it can be collected, leading to a significant boost in brightness and a faster emission rate.

This work is interesting because it brings us closer to building efficient interfaces in silicon that can connect light and quantum information, potentially leading to more powerful quantum computers and secure communication systems built on familiar silicon technology.

Background

Quantum computing and secure communication rely on "qubits," which are like tiny switches that can be both on and off at the same time. To build these technologies, we need reliable ways to create and control these qubits. Silicon is a fantastic material for building complex electronics, so scientists are keen to make qubits directly within silicon chips. One promising candidate for a silicon-based qubit is called a "T center." However, T centers are currently quite dim emitters of light, meaning they don't send out many useful photons (particles of light). This dimness makes them difficult to work with for practical applications. The core problem this research addresses is how to make these T centers much brighter and more efficient light emitters.

What they did

The researchers tackled the dimness of T centers by embedding them within a specially designed nanophotonic cavity. Imagine a tiny, precisely shaped structure made of silicon that acts like a miniature echo chamber for light. This cavity is designed to resonate at the specific color of light that a T center emits. When a T center emits a photon, the cavity can "trap" that photon for a short time, increasing the probability that it will be re-emitted in a desired direction and at the desired wavelength. This process, known as the Purcell effect, dramatically enhances the emission rate. The researchers built these cavities using a nanobeam structure, which is essentially a tiny silicon beam with precisely placed holes. This structure is designed not only to enhance emission but also to efficiently guide the emitted light into an optical fiber, which is crucial for collecting the photons. They then fabricated these cavities and implanted T centers into them. Finally, they carefully measured the light emitted by single T centers within these cavities.

What they found

The results show a dramatic improvement in the brightness of the T center's emission. Specifically, the zero-phonon line (the most useful, pure emission from the T center) became two orders of magnitude brighter compared to T centers coupled to waveguides (which are like tiny light pipes). They also achieved a very good efficiency of 23% for collecting the emitted light from the T center into an optical fiber. Overall, the efficiency of emission into the zero-phonon line reached an impressive 63.4%. Furthermore, the researchers observed that the T center's excited state lifetime (how long it stays in an excited state before emitting light) was reduced by a factor of 5. This reduction in lifetime is a direct sign of the Purcell effect at work, indicating a "Purcell factor" exceeding 18 when accounting for all emission pathways. They also demonstrated that applying a magnetic field, which is important for controlling spin qubits, tunes the T center out of resonance with the cavity, causing its brightness to decrease and its lifetime to increase, confirming the strong coupling between the T center and the cavity.

Why it matters

This research represents a significant step towards realizing silicon-based quantum technologies. By dramatically increasing the brightness and collection efficiency of T centers, these findings pave the way for building efficient "spin-photon interfaces." These interfaces are essential for quantum networks, where information encoded in the spin of a T center needs to be reliably converted into photons for transmission, and for distributed quantum computing, where qubits need to communicate with each other. While the current experiments were performed at cryogenic temperatures (3.6 Kelvin), which can broaden the emission linewidth, the researchers are optimistic that further improvements, such as operating at lower temperatures and using isotopically purified silicon, could lead to even higher efficiency and indistinguishable photons – a key requirement for many quantum applications. The ability to create these bright, controllable light sources directly in silicon makes them highly attractive for future scalable quantum technologies.
