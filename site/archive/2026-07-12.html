Microwave-based quantum control and coherence protection of tin-vacancy spin qubits in a strain-tuned diamond membrane heterostructure

Xinghan Guo, Alexander M. Stramma, Zixi Li, William G. Roth, Benchen Huang, Yu Jin et al.
The Brief

This research demonstrates a novel way to control and protect the delicate quantum states of defects in diamond, paving the way for better quantum technologies.

The core innovation involves using carefully engineered strain in thin diamond membranes to improve the performance of tin-vacancy (SnV) centers, which are like tiny quantum bits. This strain allows for precise control of the SnV's spin using microwaves, achieving very high accuracy, and crucially, it shields the spin from environmental noise, extending its useful "coherence time" (how long it can hold quantum information) to over a millisecond even at slightly warmer temperatures. This strain also helps maintain the clarity of the light signals emitted by these defects.

This breakthrough is exciting because it brings us closer to building robust quantum networks and sensors that can operate at more accessible temperatures, making advanced quantum technologies more practical.

Background

Quantum technologies, like quantum computers and secure communication networks, rely on building blocks called "qubits." These qubits need to be stable, controllable, and able to interact with light (photons) to transmit quantum information. Diamond has a defect called the tin-vacancy center (SnV) that shows promise as a qubit. It has good optical properties and can store quantum information for a decent amount of time, especially at very cold temperatures (around 1.7 Kelvin, which is -271.45 degrees Celsius). However, two major hurdles prevent its widespread use: it's difficult to precisely control the SnV's spin state using microwaves, and its performance degrades significantly as temperatures rise, making it impractical for many common cryogenic systems.

What they did

This research tackles these challenges by creating a new platform for SnV qubits: diamond membranes with built-in strain. They started with thin diamond membranes containing SnV centers. These membranes were then bonded to a different material, fused silica, using a special adhesive. The key idea is that diamond and fused silica expand and contract differently with temperature. By heating the combined structure and then cooling it down to very low temperatures, the diamond membrane is stretched, creating a uniform, internal "strain" (a controlled deformation) within the diamond. This strain is crucial because it alters the electronic structure of the SnV center. Specifically, it causes "orbital mixing," which makes the SnV's spin state much more responsive to microwave signals. They also fabricated tiny gold wires (coplanar waveguides) on top of the diamond to deliver these microwave signals precisely to the SnV.

What they found

The introduction of strain had remarkable effects. First, it enabled precise control of the SnV's spin state using microwaves, achieving a high "gate fidelity" of 99.36(9)%. This means that when they sent a microwave pulse to flip the qubit's state, it did so correctly almost every time. Second, the strain significantly protected the qubit's quantum information from degrading, extending its "coherence time" (how long it can maintain its quantum state) beyond a millisecond at 1.7 K. Crucially, this strain also suppressed temperature-dependent noise that usually causes the qubit to lose its quantum information. As a result, even at a more accessible temperature of 4 Kelvin (-269.15 degrees Celsius), the SnV maintained a coherence time of 223(10) microseconds. Furthermore, the optical properties of the SnV, which are vital for interacting with light, remained excellent, showing very narrow spectral lines even at these elevated temperatures.

Why it matters

This work presents a significant step forward for using SnV centers as practical quantum components. By overcoming the limitations of microwave control and temperature sensitivity, this strained diamond membrane platform makes SnV qubits much more viable for real-world quantum technologies. The ability to operate effectively at 4 Kelvin is a game-changer, as 4 K cryogenic systems are far more common, affordable, and easier to manage than the ultra-low temperature systems previously required. This opens the door for building more robust and scalable quantum networks and sensors. While the coherence times are impressive, future work could further optimize the strain generation and control techniques to push these values even higher. The compatibility of diamond membranes with other fabrication techniques also suggests a clear path towards integrating these qubits into more complex quantum devices.
