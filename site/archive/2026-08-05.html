Approximability and Rouquier dimension for noncommutative algebras over schemes

Timothy De Deyn, Pat Lank, Kabeer Manali Rahul
The Brief

This research explores how well we can "approximate" certain mathematical structures called noncommutative algebras, using a concept called Rouquier dimension.

The core finding is that a specific type of approximation, known as strong generation, for these algebras over geometric spaces (schemes) happens precisely when the algebra has a finite "global dimension" across its entire structure, or at least when we can cover the space with simpler pieces (affine open covers) where this finite global dimension holds. This directly answers a long-standing question in the field. As an immediate benefit, the work clarifies when certain special types of algebras, called Azumaya algebras, possess these essential "generators."

This work is interesting because it connects abstract algebraic structures to geometric properties, offering a deeper understanding of how these mathematical objects are built and how they behave, with implications for fields like algebraic geometry and representation theory.

Background

Mathematicians often study geometric spaces by looking at algebraic structures associated with them. For a long time, this was mostly done for "commutative" structures, where multiplication behaves like ordinary numbers (e.g., $a \times b = b \times a$). However, many interesting mathematical objects and physical phenomena are better described by "noncommutative" structures, where multiplication does not necessarily commute. This paper tackles a fundamental question in this "noncommutative algebraic geometry": how can we understand the "size" and "complexity" of these noncommutative structures, particularly when viewed through the lens of "triangulated categories"? These categories are powerful tools for organizing mathematical objects and their relationships, and understanding their properties is key to unlocking deeper insights. Specifically, the researchers were motivated by a desire to extend existing techniques for analyzing commutative algebraic structures to their noncommutative counterparts, addressing a problem posed by Amnon Neeman.

What they did

The core of this work is about understanding "generators" and "approximability" in specialized mathematical structures called "triangulated categories" that arise from noncommutative algebras attached to geometric spaces (called "schemes"). Think of a triangulated category as a sophisticated way to organize mathematical objects and their relationships, like building blocks. A "generator" is a fundamental building block from which all other objects in the category can be constructed. A "strong generator" is even more powerful, allowing construction within a limited number of steps. The "Rouquier dimension" is a measure of how complex these constructions are, specifically the minimum number of steps needed.

The researchers introduced the concept of "mild noncommutative schemes," which are geometric spaces equipped with a noncommutative structure sheaf. They then focused on the "category of perfect complexes" associated with these noncommutative schemes. A key technique they employed is "approximability," which allows understanding complex objects by approximating them with simpler ones. They established a crucial equivalence: a noncommutative structure on a geometric space has a "perfect strong $\oplus$-generator" (a type of fundamental building block) if and only if, when you look at its "affine open covers" (essentially, breaking down the space into simpler, flat pieces), the associated algebraic structure has "finite global dimension" (a property related to how well its elements can be resolved by simpler algebraic objects). This equivalence is a central technical achievement.

What they found

The main result is a set of equivalent conditions that determine when a specific triangulated category, called $DQcoh(A)$, associated with a noncommutative algebra $A$ over a scheme $X$, has a "perfect strong $\oplus$-generator." These conditions are: 1. $DQcoh(A)$ has a perfect strong $\oplus$-generator. 2. For every "affine open" part of the scheme $X$, the corresponding category $DQcoh(A|_U)$ has a perfect strong $\oplus$-generator. 3. The algebra $A(U)$, which represents the sections of $A$ over an affine open set $U$, has finite global dimension. 4. For any affine open cover of $X$ by sets $U_i$, the algebras $A(U_i)$ have finite global dimension.

Furthermore, any of these conditions imply that the category of "perfect complexes" associated with $A$, denoted $perf(A)$, has a finite Rouquier dimension. This is a significant generalization of a known result for commutative schemes.

As a direct application, they showed that for a "separated Noetherian scheme" $X$, the following are equivalent: 1. The category $perf(X)$ has finite Rouquier dimension (meaning $X$ is regular of finite Krull dimension). 2. The category $perf(A)$ has finite Rouquier dimension for *all* "Azumaya algebras" $A$ over $X$. (Azumaya algebras are a special class of noncommutative algebras related to the Brauer group of a scheme). 3. The category $perf(A)$ has finite Rouquier dimension for *some* Azumaya algebra $A$ over $X$.

Why it matters

This work bridges a gap between the study of commutative and noncommutative algebraic geometry. By providing concrete criteria for the existence of generators and bounding the Rouquier dimension in noncommutative settings, it allows mathematicians to apply powerful tools developed for commutative geometry to a much broader class of objects. This is crucial for understanding the structure and properties of noncommutative spaces, which appear in various areas of mathematics and physics. The solution to Neeman's problem and the characterization of regularity for Azumaya algebras are significant advancements. Essentially, this research makes complex noncommutative structures more accessible and analyzable, paving the way for new discoveries in algebraic geometry and related fields. It demonstrates that many properties previously understood only in commutative settings can be generalized, offering a unified perspective.
