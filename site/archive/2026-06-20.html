Distributed Quantum Computing across an Optical Network Link

D. Main, P. Drmota, D. P. Nadlinger, E. M. Ainley, A. Agrawal, B. C. Nichol et al.
The Brief

This research demonstrates a crucial step towards building larger quantum computers by linking smaller ones together over a network.

The core idea is to use light (photons) to connect separate quantum processors, specifically trapped ions, and then use these connections to perform quantum operations between them. They achieved this by creating entanglement, a special quantum link, between "network qubits" in each processor. This entanglement allowed them to "teleport" a quantum gate, a fundamental building block of quantum computation, between "circuit qubits" in different locations with high accuracy. This is the first time this crucial teleportation step has been done reliably and repeatably.

This work is exciting because it shows a practical way to combine quantum computing power from different physical locations, paving the way for more powerful quantum computers that aren't limited by the size of a single chip.

Background

Building a powerful quantum computer is incredibly challenging. One major hurdle is scaling up the number of quantum bits (qubits) while maintaining precise control and the ability for them to interact. Imagine trying to build a supercomputer where every single component needs to be perfectly connected and controlled – it quickly becomes unmanageable. This research tackles this problem by proposing a "distributed" approach. Instead of one massive quantum processor, the idea is to link together several smaller, more manageable quantum processing modules. This way, the complexity is spread out, and the challenge shifts from building one giant machine to building many smaller ones and connecting them effectively.

What they did

This paper demonstrates a way to make these smaller quantum modules work together as if they were one larger quantum computer. They use trapped ions – charged atoms held in place by electromagnetic fields – as their qubits. Specifically, they have two separate modules, about 2 meters apart, each containing two types of ions: strontium (Sr+) and calcium (Ca+).

The Sr+ ion acts as a "network qubit," which is good at sending and receiving quantum information through light (photons). The Ca+ ion serves as a "circuit qubit," where the actual quantum computation happens. To connect these modules, they first create entanglement between the network qubits in each module. Entanglement is a peculiar quantum phenomenon where two particles become linked, sharing the same fate no matter how far apart they are. They achieve this by sending photons from each Sr+ ion to a central point where their interference creates entanglement between the two ions. This process is "heralded," meaning they get a signal when the entanglement is successfully created.

Once the network qubits are entangled, they use a technique called Quantum Gate Teleportation (QGT) to perform operations on the circuit qubits in different modules. QGT allows a quantum gate (a fundamental operation in quantum computing) to be executed between two qubits that are not physically close, using the shared entanglement and some classical communication. They specifically teleport a "controlled-Z" (CZ) gate, which is a crucial two-qubit gate. They also show they can perform more complex operations, like iSWAP and SWAP gates, by combining multiple QGTs. Finally, they demonstrate this by running a simplified version of Grover's search algorithm, a well-known quantum algorithm for searching unsorted databases.

What they found

The researchers successfully teleported a CZ gate between circuit qubits in separate modules with a fidelity of 86%. Fidelity is a measure of how accurately the operation was performed, with 100% being perfect. They then executed a distributed version of Grover's search algorithm, which involved multiple QGTs, achieving a 71% success rate. They also demonstrated the ability to perform distributed iSWAP and SWAP gates, which required 2 and 3 instances of QGT, respectively, showing the flexibility of their approach for arbitrary two-qubit operations.

Why it matters

This work is significant because it's the first demonstration of deterministic Quantum Gate Teleportation across a quantum network. "Deterministic" means the operation reliably succeeds, which is crucial for building scalable quantum computers. Previously, such operations were often probabilistic, meaning they might fail and require many attempts. This breakthrough provides a viable pathway for building larger, more powerful quantum computers by connecting smaller, modular units. The use of photons as the communication channel makes this approach versatile, potentially applicable to other types of quantum computing hardware beyond trapped ions, such as those based on diamond defects or neutral atoms. This opens the door to a future where quantum computers are built from interconnected modules, much like classical supercomputers are built from interconnected processors. While the current system has limitations in terms of fidelity and the number of qubits per module, the demonstrated deterministic QGT and successful execution of a distributed quantum algorithm are major steps towards realizing large-scale quantum computing.
