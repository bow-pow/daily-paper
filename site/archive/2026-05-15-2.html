Bigger, Regularized, Optimistic: scaling for compute and sample-efficient continuous control

Michal Nauman, Mateusz Ostaszewski, Krzysztof Jankowski, Piotr Miłoś, Marek Cygan
The Brief

This research explores how making Reinforcement Learning (RL) systems bigger and smarter can dramatically improve their ability to learn complex tasks. The core idea is that by using strong "regularization" – techniques that prevent the learning system (specifically, its "critic networks" which evaluate actions) from becoming too complex and overfitting – we can effectively scale up these networks. This, combined with "optimistic exploration" (a strategy that encourages trying out new actions), allows the BRO algorithm to learn much more efficiently and achieve near-perfect results on challenging control problems. This work is interesting because it shows that simply scaling up existing methods, when done thoughtfully with specific improvements, can unlock significant performance gains, potentially leading to more capable AI in areas like robotics and game playing.

Background

Imagine teaching a robot to perform a complex physical task, like walking or picking up an object. Reinforcement learning (RL) is a way to do this by having the robot learn through trial and error, receiving rewards for good actions and penalties for bad ones. Traditionally, researchers focused on creating smarter algorithms to make this learning process faster and more efficient, meaning the robot could learn to perform the task with fewer trials. However, this paper asks a different question: what if we could achieve significant improvements not just by making the learning *algorithm* smarter, but by making the *learning system* bigger and more capable? Specifically, they wanted to see if scaling up the neural networks used to represent the robot's "brain" (its policy and value functions) could lead to better and faster learning in continuous control tasks, where actions are smooth movements like adjusting a joint angle.

What they did

The researchers proposed a new algorithm called BRO (Bigger, Regularized, Optimistic). Their core idea is that simply making neural networks bigger doesn't automatically work; in fact, it can sometimes make things worse. To successfully scale up the "critic" network (which estimates the value of being in a certain state and taking a certain action), they found two key ingredients were crucial: strong

regularization

and

optimistic exploration

.

Regularization refers to techniques that prevent a neural network from becoming too specialized to the training data, which can lead to poor performance on new situations. In BRO, they used a custom architecture called "BroNet" which incorporates Layer Normalization (a technique that helps stabilize training) after each layer, similar to how modern large language models are built. They also used weight decay (a standard regularization technique) and full-parameter resets.

Optimistic exploration is about encouraging the agent to try new things. Instead of just sticking to what it knows, it's nudged to explore actions that *might* lead to very good outcomes, even if they seem risky. BRO uses a "dual actor" setup where one policy is used for exploration and another for learning. This optimistic exploration is combined with a way of estimating values that avoids being overly pessimistic, which is a common issue in RL that can slow down learning.

They extensively tested BRO against existing state-of-the-art methods across 40 complex continuous control tasks from benchmarks like DeepMind Control, MetaWorld, and MyoSuite. They compared BRO not only in terms of final performance but also in

sample efficiency

(how quickly it learns) and

wallclock time

(how long it takes to train on a computer).

What they found

BRO achieved state-of-the-art results, significantly outperforming leading model-based and model-free algorithms on these challenging tasks. Notably, BRO was the first model-free algorithm to achieve near-optimal performance on notoriously difficult "Dog" and "Humanoid" locomotion tasks. For example, on these challenging tasks, BRO achieved performance levels that a leading model-based algorithm (TD-MPC2) only reached after requiring approximately 2.5 times more training steps.

They found that scaling up the critic network, particularly with their BroNet architecture and strong regularization, was far more impactful than scaling up the actor network (which decides which action to take). They also discovered that increasing the "replay ratio" (how many learning updates are performed per environment interaction) had diminishing returns compared to scaling the network size. In fact, a larger network with a lower replay ratio could be more computationally efficient and achieve better performance than a smaller network with a high replay ratio. A key finding was that disabling a common technique called Clipped Double Q-learning (CDQ), which is used to prevent overestimation of values, actually *improved* performance when combined with their regularization techniques, suggesting that regularization can handle value overestimation effectively.

Why it matters

This research challenges the long-held belief that sample efficiency in RL is solely about algorithmic cleverness. It demonstrates that scaling up the capacity of the learning model, specifically the critic network, when combined with appropriate regularization and exploration strategies, can lead to substantial gains in performance and sample efficiency. This has broad implications for developing more capable robots and autonomous systems that can learn complex physical skills faster and with less data. The success of BRO on challenging tasks like controlling a simulated humanoid robot is a significant step forward.

However, BRO's larger models require more memory, which could be a limitation for real-time applications with strict computational constraints. Future work might focus on making these large models more efficient for deployment. The paper also highlights the need for new benchmarks that specifically test the sample efficiency of modern RL algorithms, as current benchmarks might not fully capture the capabilities of methods like BRO. The BroNet architecture itself also showed promise in other areas like offline reinforcement learning, suggesting its potential beyond continuous control.
