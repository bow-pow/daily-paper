Profile Likelihoods in Cosmology: When, Why and How illustrated with $Λ$CDM, Massive Neutrinos and Dark Energy

Laura Herold, Elisa G. M. Ferreira, Lukas Heinrich
The Brief

This research explores a statistical tool called the "profile likelihood" for understanding the universe's fundamental properties. The profile likelihood is a way for scientists to estimate the values of cosmological parameters, like how much dark matter or dark energy there is, using data from telescopes. The study investigates when this method reliably provides accurate estimates, especially when dealing with complex models of the universe, and finds that it works well for the standard cosmological model but can be less reliable when considering things like the mass of neutrinos or the behavior of dark energy. This work is interesting because it helps ensure that the tools used to interpret cosmic data are accurate, leading to more trustworthy conclusions about the universe's composition and evolution.

Background

Cosmology, the study of the universe's origin, evolution, and large-scale structure, has become a highly precise science. This precision is built upon sophisticated statistical methods used to analyze vast amounts of observational data, like that from the Planck satellite measuring the cosmic microwave background (CMB). The standard model of cosmology, known as Lambda-CDM (ΛCDM), describes the universe's composition and evolution. However, as cosmologists push the boundaries to understand mysterious components like dark energy and dark matter, and to resolve discrepancies in measurements (like the "Hubble tension"), they introduce new parameters into their models. This complexity makes statistical analysis challenging. This research addresses a specific statistical technique, the "profile likelihood," and questions when it can be reliably used to draw conclusions about these cosmological parameters, especially when compared to the more commonly used Bayesian methods.

What they did

The paper focuses on "frequentist" statistical inference, a framework that contrasts with the more familiar "Bayesian" approach. In frequentist statistics, the emphasis is on the properties of a statistical procedure over many hypothetical repetitions of an experiment. A key concept is the "confidence interval," which is designed to contain the true value of a parameter a certain percentage of the time (e.g., 95% of the time). The researchers investigate a specific method for constructing these intervals called the "graphical profile likelihood." This method is an approximation that works well when there's a lot of data, a situation described by "Wilks' theorem." Wilks' theorem states that under certain conditions (specifically, with a large dataset), a particular statistical quantity derived from the likelihood function will follow a known probability distribution (the chi-squared distribution).

The core of the work is to test whether the assumptions behind the graphical profile likelihood method hold true in realistic cosmological scenarios. They use data from the Planck 2018 mission, specifically a simplified version called "Plik lite," which is easier to simulate. They examine three cosmological models: 1.

ΛCDM

The standard model. 2.

ΛCDM + Mν

The standard model extended to include the total mass of neutrinos as a free parameter. Neutrinos are known to have mass, but their exact contribution to the universe's mass budget is uncertain. 3.

w₀CDM

The standard model extended to include a parameter describing the equation of state of dark energy (w₀), which dictates how its pressure relates to its density.

To test the validity of the graphical profile likelihood, they generate many simulated datasets ("mocks") based on these models and the Planck data. They then analyze these mocks to see if the statistical quantities behave as predicted by Wilks' theorem and related theories (like "Wald's relation," which describes the relationship between estimated parameters and the likelihood). They also investigate situations where parameters are close to physical boundaries (e.g., a mass cannot be negative) and how this affects the method.

What they found

The researchers found that the graphical profile likelihood method works well for the basic ΛCDM model, providing confidence intervals with correct coverage (meaning they reliably capture the true parameter value the stated percentage of the time). However, when they introduced the sum of neutrino masses as a free parameter (ΛCDM + Mν), they found indications that the assumptions of Wilks' theorem might be violated. This suggests that the standard graphical profile likelihood might not provide correct coverage in this case, and a more complex method, like the "boundary-corrected" or "Feldman-Cousins" construction, might be needed. For the w₀CDM model, which includes dark energy's equation of state as a free parameter, they found stronger evidence that the assumptions of the graphical profile likelihood are violated. This means that the simple graphical method might lead to misleading constraints for this model. They also developed and released a new code called "pinc" to help compute profile likelihoods in cosmology.

Why it matters

This research is important because it highlights the need for caution when applying statistical methods in cosmology. While Bayesian methods are widely used and often provide reliable results, frequentist methods like profile likelihoods offer complementary information and can be particularly useful in specific situations, such as when prior beliefs might unduly influence Bayesian results or when parameters are near physical boundaries. However, the validity of these frequentist methods, especially the commonly used graphical profile likelihood, relies on assumptions that are not always met in complex cosmological models.

The findings suggest that cosmologists should carefully check whether the conditions for the graphical profile likelihood are satisfied before relying on its results, especially for models beyond the simplest ΛCDM. If the assumptions are violated, as seen in the cases with massive neutrinos and evolving dark energy, the derived confidence intervals might not be accurate. This could lead to incorrect conclusions about the universe's properties. The paper's contribution is to provide a rigorous assessment of these assumptions and to offer tools (like their "pinc" code) to help researchers perform these checks, ultimately leading to more robust and reliable cosmological inferences. For a non-specialist, the takeaway is that even in seemingly straightforward scientific measurements, the statistical tools used are complex and require careful validation to ensure the conclusions drawn are sound.
