Classical surrogate simulation of quantum systems with LOWESA

Manuel S. Rudolph, Enrico Fontana, Zoë Holmes, Lukasz Cincio
The Brief

This research introduces a new classical method called LOWESA for simulating quantum systems, which are notoriously difficult for regular computers to model accurately. LOWESA works by first creating a "surrogate expectation landscape," essentially a pre-computed map of how quantum systems behave under different conditions. Once this map is built, it allows for incredibly fast predictions of quantum system behavior for many different scenarios, even for large systems like the 127-qubit Ising model. This means researchers can quickly explore a vast range of quantum problems without needing a powerful quantum computer for every single test. This could be interesting because it offers a faster, more accessible way to understand and design new quantum technologies by allowing for rapid exploration of many potential quantum setups on standard computers.

Background

Simulating quantum systems is incredibly difficult for classical computers. Even with the most powerful supercomputers, accurately predicting the behavior of even moderately sized quantum systems is often impossible. This is because the complexity of quantum systems grows exponentially with the number of particles (like qubits in a quantum computer). Researchers are trying to find ways to use classical computers to simulate these systems more efficiently, especially to help design and understand future quantum computers and to explore complex physical phenomena that are hard to study experimentally. This paper tackles the challenge of simulating a specific type of quantum system, the transverse-field Ising model, which is relevant for understanding magnetism and is also a benchmark for quantum computing experiments. The core problem is how to get accurate predictions for the expected outcome of a quantum measurement (an "expectation value") when the system's behavior depends on various adjustable parameters, like magnetic field strengths or interaction strengths between particles.

What they did

This research introduces a new classical algorithm called LOWESA (Low-Weight Simulation Algorithm). Instead of simulating a quantum system step-by-step for each specific set of parameters, LOWESA builds a "surrogate expectation landscape." Think of this landscape as a smooth, predictable surface that approximates the true, complex behavior of the quantum system across all possible parameter values.

Here's how it works: 1.

Decomposition into basic operations

Any quantum circuit (a sequence of operations performed on qubits) can be broken down into simpler building blocks. LOWESA specifically focuses on a structure involving "Clifford operations" (which are relatively easy to simulate classically) and parameterized single-qubit "Z-rotation gates." These Z-rotation gates are where the adjustable parameters come in. 2.

Pauli Transfer Matrix (PTM) formalism

The algorithm uses a mathematical framework where quantum operators (like those representing measurements) and states are represented as vectors of coefficients corresponding to different combinations of basic quantum operations called Pauli operators (I, X, Y, Z). 3.

Path-based decomposition

The key idea is that each Z-rotation gate can split a "path" of calculation into two. This creates a tree-like structure of possible calculation paths. The final expectation value is a sum of contributions from all these paths, weighted by trigonometric functions (sine and cosine) that depend on the rotation angles (parameters). 4.

Building the surrogate landscape

Instead of calculating every single path, which can be computationally overwhelming, LOWESA approximates this sum. It does this by using "truncation" techniques. These techniques involve intelligently deciding which paths are most important to include and which can be ignored without losing too much accuracy. For example, they might limit the "frequency" of paths considered (meaning paths that oscillate too rapidly are discarded) or prune paths based on the complexity of the quantum operator they are tracking. This process creates a classical "surrogate" function that represents the entire landscape of possible expectation values for a range of parameters. 5.

Rapid evaluation

Once this surrogate landscape is built, evaluating the expectation value for any specific set of parameters becomes extremely fast, often taking just fractions of a second. This is a significant advantage over traditional methods that would need to re-run a costly simulation for each parameter set.

What they found

The researchers applied LOWESA to simulate a 127-qubit transverse-field Ising model on a heavy-hexagon lattice, a system that was recently studied experimentally. They were able to reconstruct the entire expectation landscape for this system.

Specifically: * They simulated up to 20 "Trotter steps" (discrete time steps in simulating quantum evolution) for the 127-qubit system. * The initial overhead to build the surrogate landscape took "minutes to hours on a laptop," depending on the complexity. * Once the landscape was built, evaluating the expectation value for specific parameter settings (like different magnetic field strengths or coupling strengths) took "fractions of a second on a laptop." * They demonstrated that for some simple cases, the expectation curves could be accurately reproduced by basic trigonometric functions like sin²(5θ) and sin(3θ/4), which they identified by examining the "trivial paths" in their calculation. * For more complex scenarios, including circuits with over double the number of parameters compared to previous studies, LOWESA produced results that were in high agreement with exact calculations where possible and also matched experimental results from a recent Nature paper (Ref. [21]).

Why it matters

LOWESA offers a powerful new way to classically simulate quantum systems, especially when exploring a wide range of parameters. Its key advantage is the ability to build a comprehensive "surrogate landscape" that can then be queried very quickly. This is a departure from traditional methods that typically simulate one specific configuration at a time, which can be very slow if you need to explore many different configurations.

For non-specialists, the takeaway is that while building a full quantum computer is a long-term goal, classical computing is still vital for quantum research. LOWESA represents a significant step forward in using classical computers to understand and predict quantum behavior. It can accelerate the process of finding optimal parameters for quantum algorithms, exploring new physical phenomena, and benchmarking quantum hardware. The ability to rapidly scan entire "landscapes" of possibilities means researchers can explore more scenarios, discover new physics, and design better quantum devices more efficiently. While it has limitations (like any classical simulation method, it doesn't guarantee perfect accuracy within a fixed budget), its speed and flexibility make it a promising tool for the future of quantum simulation.
