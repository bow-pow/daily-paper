HARP: Hadamard-Preconditioned Adaptive Rotation Processor for Extreme LLM Quantization

Artur Zagitov, Gleb Molodtsov, Aleksandr Beznosikov
The Brief

This research presents a new method called HARP to make very large language models (LLMs) much smaller and faster without losing much accuracy.

The core idea is to cleverly rearrange the numbers (weights and activations) inside the LLM before quantizing them, which is the process of reducing their precision to save space. Unlike previous methods that use a fixed, random rearrangement, HARP learns the best rearrangement for each part of the model, adapting to the specific data and hardware. This adaptive rearrangement, which is structured like a series of interconnected blocks, helps overcome the challenges of extreme low-bit quantization, especially when dealing with unusual data values or complex relationships within the model's parameters.

This matters because it could lead to LLMs that are significantly faster and require much less memory, making powerful AI models accessible on a wider range of devices, from powerful servers to potentially even personal computers, and dramatically speeding up their response times.

Background

Deploying Large Language Models (LLMs) like ChatGPT is challenging because they are incredibly large and require a lot of memory and processing power. To make them more accessible and efficient, researchers try to reduce the number of bits used to represent the model's parameters (weights) and intermediate calculations (activations). This process is called quantization. However, when you try to use very few bits (e.g., 2 to 4 bits), a common technique called Post-Training Quantization (PTQ) becomes very sensitive to "outliers" (unusually large values) and the "anisotropic weight curvature" (the way the model's weights change unevenly in different directions). These issues can significantly degrade the model's performance.

What they did

This research introduces a new method called HARP (Hadamard-Preconditioned Adaptive Rotation Processor) to improve extreme low-bit quantization for LLMs. The core idea is to transform the model's weights and activations into a different "coordinate system" before quantizing them. This transformation is done using a special mathematical operation called an orthogonal rotation.

Previously, researchers used fixed, randomized Hadamard transforms (RHTs) for this rotation. While RHTs help make quantization more robust, they are not tailored to the specific LLM layer, the data being processed, or the particular way the quantization is done. HARP improves upon this by creating a *learnable* rotation processor. Instead of a fixed RHT, HARP uses a structured, multi-stage rotation that can be adjusted based on the LLM's data during a calibration phase. This means HARP learns the best way to "mix" the weights and activations for each specific layer and quantization setup.

HARP is designed to be a "drop-in" replacement for RHT. It starts by initializing itself to be very similar to an RHT, providing a strong baseline. Then, it refines this rotation using only a small amount of calibration data. The paper emphasizes that HARP maintains *exact full-precision equivalence*, meaning the mathematical behavior of the model remains unchanged before quantization, which is crucial for preserving accuracy. HARP achieves this efficiency by breaking down the complex rotation into a series of simpler, structured stages, similar to how fast Fourier transforms work. It also supports dimensions that aren't powers of two, which are common in LLMs.

What they found

Experiments show that HARP consistently improves the performance of LLMs quantized to 2-4 bits compared to using fixed RHTs. For instance, when quantizing to 2 bits, HARP significantly reduces the "perplexity" (a measure of how well a language model predicts text, where lower is better) on models ranging from 1 billion to 70 billion parameters. On the Llama 2 7B model at 2 bits, HARP achieved a perplexity of 7.23 on WikiText2, compared to 8.22 for RHT. This improvement translates to better zero-shot accuracy on various tasks as well. Importantly, HARP does this while largely preserving the speed benefits of quantization. For example, on a 7B parameter model, HARP achieved 128 tokens per second, which is significantly faster than the 61 tokens per second of unquantized FP16, and only slightly slower than the 142 tokens per second of RHT. The paper also notes that HARP's learned parameters can be stored compactly using 8-bit integers with minimal loss in performance.

Why it matters

HARP offers a way to make LLMs much more efficient for deployment, especially on devices with limited memory and processing power, without sacrificing accuracy. By learning a more effective way to prepare the model's data for extreme low-bit quantization, HARP helps bridge the performance gap between highly compressed models and their full-precision counterparts. This is particularly important for making advanced AI models more accessible and deployable in real-world applications. While HARP requires a calibration step (which adds some computational cost), the benefits in terms of model performance and inference speed are substantial. The research also highlights that the choice of how to transform data before quantization is critical and can be learned to achieve better results, making HARP a valuable tool for future LLM optimization efforts.
