A General Model for Dark Energy Crossing the Phantom Divide

Zhibang Yao, Gen Ye, Alessandra Silvestri
The Brief

This research proposes a flexible new way to describe dark energy, the mysterious force accelerating the expansion of the universe.

The core idea is to separate how the universe's overall expansion and the tiny ripples within it are governed, using different mathematical controls. This allows for any kind of cosmic expansion while preventing problematic instabilities in the universe's structure. Crucially, it lets researchers directly study when dark energy might transition from being "normal" to "phantom" – a state where its pressure is even more negative – without getting bogged down in complex calculations. The model, nicknamed "Freezing Gravity," features a peculiar behavior where the underlying field governing dark energy effectively stops evolving on very large cosmic scales.

This work is interesting because it offers a more straightforward path to understanding the exotic possibilities of dark energy, potentially shedding light on the universe's ultimate fate.

Background

Our universe is expanding, and observations suggest this expansion is accelerating. This acceleration is attributed to a mysterious component called "dark energy" (DE). The standard model of cosmology, known as Lambda-CDM, describes dark energy as a constant energy density, akin to Einstein's cosmological constant. However, recent observational data, particularly from the Dark Energy Spectroscopic Instrument (DESI), hint that dark energy might not be constant. Instead, its properties could be changing over time, potentially even crossing a critical threshold known as the "phantom divide." This divide is significant because crossing it implies unusual behavior for dark energy, and it's notoriously difficult to achieve in many theoretical models without causing problematic instabilities. The motivation for this research is to develop a flexible theoretical framework that can accommodate these evolving dark energy properties, specifically the possibility of crossing the phantom divide, while remaining physically stable.

What they did

The researchers propose a new theoretical model for dark energy, which they call "Freezing Gravity." They build this model within a broad class of theories called spatially covariant theories, which are designed to be consistent with general relativity but allow for more complex gravitational interactions. The key innovation is to separate the control over the universe's overall expansion (the "background evolution") from the control over small-scale fluctuations or "perturbations" (like the growth of galaxies). This separation is achieved by introducing different sets of coefficients in the mathematical description of the theory, derived from a fundamental quantity called the Lagrangian, which encodes the laws of physics.

Crucially, in this model, the equation of state of dark energy, which describes its pressure-to-density ratio (denoted by 'w'), is directly determined by two simple functions of time, rather than being an indirect consequence of solving complex equations of motion. This direct control allows them to engineer any desired background evolution, including crossing the phantom divide (where 'w' goes from being greater than -1 to less than -1), without needing to first figure out how the universe's expansion unfolds. To ensure the model is physically viable, they rigorously analyze the stability of linear perturbations. This involves checking for "ghost instabilities" (where particles with negative energy appear, leading to unphysical behavior) and "gradient instabilities" (where perturbations grow uncontrollably). They find that their model can avoid these instabilities by carefully choosing the coefficients that govern perturbations. A unique feature they identify is that on very large scales (the "infrared" or IR limit), the dark energy field becomes "non-dynamical," meaning it essentially stops evolving. They speculate that this "freezing" behavior, even though it can sometimes signal problems in other theories, is safe here because it happens at all levels of perturbation, not just the simplest ones.

What they found

The core finding is the construction of the "Freezing Gravity" model, which allows for arbitrary dark energy equation of state evolutions, including crossing the phantom divide, without needing to solve background equations of motion. This is achieved by making the equation of state directly dependent on two free functions of time, F(t) and G(t). Stability of linear perturbations is controlled by a separate set of coefficients, σ1, ρ1, and ρ2, which are functions of time and the lapse function (related to the rate of time flow).

They found that on large scales, the sound speed of the scalar perturbation (a type of ripple in spacetime) approaches infinity, causing the field to become non-dynamical or "frozen." This behavior is dubbed "Freezing Gravity." On smaller scales, the scalar mode propagates with a finite speed of sound. A significant finding related to stability is the existence of an energy cut-off scale, signaled by a pole in the speed of sound, which occurs when the effective Planck mass (a measure of gravity's strength in the theory) exceeds the standard Planck mass. For the case where -1 < αL < 0, they derive specific upper bounds on the parameter β3 (β3 < -6/λ * (1 + 1/αL), where λ is a parameter related to the cut-off scale) to ensure stability. For the case where αL ≥ 0, they find that ghost instability is avoided, and gradient stability imposes conditions like αL ≥ 0, 2(αH + 1)² ≥ β3 > 0, and αH ≥ ˙H/H² - ˙H/H. They also present preliminary cosmological phenomenology by using the EFTCAMB solver, showing that the model can be consistent with the ΛCDM model while exhibiting mild deviations.

Why it matters

This research offers a powerful new tool for cosmologists trying to understand dark energy. The ability to directly control the dark energy equation of state, including its potential to cross the phantom divide, is a significant step forward. This is particularly relevant given recent observational hints from DESI and other experiments that dark energy might be evolving. By providing a stable theoretical framework for such evolving dark energy, Freezing Gravity can be used to test these observational hints more robustly.

The decoupling of background evolution from perturbation stability is also a crucial insight. It means that we might be able to achieve a universe with a specific expansion history (like crossing the phantom divide) without necessarily introducing instabilities that would render the model unphysical. This highlights the importance of observational probes that can measure not just the large-scale expansion but also the small-scale structure and fluctuations in the universe to truly constrain these new theories. While the model is complex, its ability to reproduce arbitrary background evolutions and remain stable makes it a promising candidate for explaining the accelerating expansion of the universe, especially if dark energy is indeed more dynamic than previously thought.
