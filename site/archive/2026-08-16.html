Ultra-clean assembly of van der Waals heterostructures

Wendong Wang, Nicholas Clark, Matthew Hamer, Amy Carl, Endre Tovari, Sam Sullivan-Allsop et al.
The Brief

This research introduces a new, cleaner way to build advanced layered materials called van der Waals heterostructures.

Current methods use sticky plastic films to move atomically thin sheets of materials, but these leave behind unwanted residues that hurt the material's performance. This work replaces those plastics with reusable, flexible silicon nitride membranes, creating incredibly clean interfaces between layers. This polymer-free approach allows for faster, more reliable assembly and opens up possibilities for building these structures at high temperatures or even underwater, leading to materials with near-perfect electronic properties.

This breakthrough could pave the way for next-generation electronic devices and new scientific discoveries by enabling the creation of much higher-quality layered materials than previously possible.

Background

Scientists are building incredibly thin, layered materials called van der Waals (vdW) heterostructures. These are made by stacking different two-dimensional (2D) materials, like sheets of graphene or hexagonal boron nitride, one on top of another. The way these layers are stacked and how clean the interfaces between them are determines how well they work for advanced electronics and new physics. The big problem has been that all current methods for stacking these layers rely on using polymers, which are like plastics. These polymers, while helpful for handling the delicate 2D materials, leave behind residues and contaminants. This contamination acts like dirt or smudges between the layers, preventing the materials from reaching their full potential, especially for sensitive electronic and optical applications. It limits the performance and the size of the areas that work perfectly.

What they did

This research introduces a new way to assemble these vdW heterostructures that completely avoids using polymers. Instead, they use reusable, flexible membranes made of silicon nitride (SiNₓ). These membranes are coated with a thin layer of metals (tantalum, platinum, and gold) to control how well they stick to the 2D materials. This metal coating is crucial because bare SiNₓ doesn't stick well to 2D materials. By carefully choosing the thickness of the gold layer, they can tune the adhesion, allowing them to pick up and place 2D materials precisely. They call this an "inorganic assembly" method. This new approach allows them to assemble these structures in various environments and at much higher temperatures than previously possible. They demonstrated this by building heterostructures in air, in a controlled inert gas environment (like argon), and for the first time, in an ultra-high vacuum (UHV) chamber, which is an extremely clean environment with very few gas molecules. They also showed it works when the assembly happens while the materials are submerged in liquids.

What they found

The results show that this polymer-free method creates incredibly clean interfaces between the 2D layers, free from the contamination that plagued older methods. They demonstrated this by creating hBN/graphene/hBN structures with clean areas larger than 25µm × 40µm, a significant improvement. Electronic tests on graphene encapsulated this way showed very high performance, with carrier mobilities exceeding 1.0 × 10⁶ cm²V⁻¹s⁻¹ at low temperatures, limited only by the size of the crystals themselves. They also successfully built complex 8-layer heterostructures with no visible contamination bubbles, achieving over a 100-fold increase in uniform area compared to previous methods. Crucially, when they performed assembly in UHV, they achieved an even higher level of cleanliness. This UHV assembly allowed for the creation of twisted graphene structures with unprecedented uniformity in their twist angle, varying by only 0.016° over 10 µm, an order of magnitude improvement. This extreme cleanliness is essential for studying exotic quantum phenomena. They also showed the technique works for large-area, chemical vapor deposition (CVD)-grown materials, which are important for manufacturing.

Why it matters

This research offers a significant leap forward in the fabrication of advanced 2D materials. By eliminating polymers, they've removed a major bottleneck that has limited the performance and applications of vdW heterostructures for years. The ability to create ultra-clean interfaces means that the ultimate performance of these devices will now be dictated by the intrinsic quality of the 2D materials themselves, not by the imperfections of the assembly process. This opens doors for realizing the full potential of these materials in next-generation electronics, quantum computing, and advanced optical technologies. The flexibility in assembly conditions, particularly the UHV capability, is critical for working with new, air-sensitive materials and for exploring fundamental physics that requires pristine interfaces. While the current method is highly effective for research, the development of large-area laminates suggests a path towards scalable manufacturing, potentially enabling custom, high-end optoelectronic devices for specialized applications.
