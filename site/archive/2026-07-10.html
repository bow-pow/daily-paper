Is Behavior Cloning All You Need? Understanding Horizon in Imitation Learning

Dylan J. Foster, Adam Block, Dipendra Misra
The Brief

This research explores how well a simple imitation learning technique, called behavior cloning, can learn complex tasks compared to more complicated methods.

Behavior cloning learns by directly copying expert actions. Previously, it was believed that behavior cloning needed a lot more data as the task got longer (a problem called "horizon"). This work shows that with a specific way of measuring errors (logarithmic loss) and controlling the total reward, behavior cloning can actually learn tasks of any length with a reasonable amount of data, similar to more advanced methods. Even when tasks are simpler, online methods don't offer a significant advantage over this improved behavior cloning.

This is interesting because it suggests that a simpler, more accessible approach might be sufficient for many real-world problems like robotics or generating text, potentially making AI development more efficient.

Background

Imagine you want to teach a robot to perform a complex task, like walking or driving a car. Instead of trying to write down specific rules or rewards for every situation (which is incredibly difficult), you can show the robot examples of how an expert does it. This is the core idea behind imitation learning (IL). The simplest way to do this is called "behavior cloning" (BC): you just try to learn a model that predicts what action the expert took in each situation. However, previous research suggested that BC is inefficient, especially for long tasks (tasks with a long "horizon"). It seemed like you'd need a huge number of demonstrations, with the number of demonstrations growing quadratically with the task's length. This led to the development of more complex "online" IL methods that could interact with the expert, which were thought to be much better for long tasks, requiring only a linear number of demonstrations. This paper asks: is this gap between simple offline BC and complex online IL fundamental, or can we do better with simpler methods?

What they did

This research revisits the efficiency of behavior cloning, specifically using a slightly different way of measuring errors, called the "logarithmic loss" (log-loss). Instead of just counting when the learned action is different from the expert's (like in the simple BC), the log-loss penalizes incorrect predictions more heavily, especially when the model is very confident about the wrong action. This is a common technique in machine learning for tasks like language modeling. The researchers analyzed this log-loss behavior cloning (LogLossBC) in a theoretical framework, focusing on scenarios where the expert's policy is "realizable" (meaning the policy class they are using is capable of representing the expert's behavior). They developed a new mathematical analysis that looks at the entire sequence of actions (a "trajectory") rather than just individual steps. This analysis allowed them to derive performance guarantees for LogLossBC. They then specialized their findings to deterministic policies (where the expert always takes the same action in a given state) and then to stochastic policies (where the expert might take different actions with certain probabilities). They also compared their findings to existing "online" IL methods, which can ask the expert for feedback during learning.

What they found

The key finding is that behavior cloning with the logarithmic loss can be surprisingly efficient, often much more so than previously thought, especially when certain conditions are met. For deterministic experts, they showed that LogLossBC can achieve sample complexity (the number of demonstrations needed) that is *independent* of the task horizon, provided the cumulative rewards are controlled and the complexity of the policy class is managed. This is a significant improvement over the quadratic dependence previously believed. Even in the "dense reward" setting (where rewards are frequent), they showed LogLossBC can achieve *linear* dependence on the horizon, matching what was previously only known for more complex online methods. Crucially, they also proved that, for general policy classes, online IL methods don't actually offer an advantage over this improved offline LogLossBC, even in simple scenarios. For stochastic experts, the situation is a bit more complex, and a quadratic dependence on horizon can be necessary in some cases. However, even here, LogLossBC can achieve linear horizon dependence under certain conditions related to the "variance" of the expert's actions.

Why it matters

This research challenges the long-held belief that simple behavior cloning is fundamentally inefficient for long tasks. It suggests that by using the logarithmic loss and carefully analyzing the problem, we can achieve sample efficiency comparable to more complex online methods, but with a much simpler offline approach. This is important because online methods require direct interaction with an expert, which can be costly or impossible in many real-world applications (like learning from pre-recorded driving data). The findings imply that for many practical imitation learning scenarios, especially those using deep neural networks (which typically have "parameter sharing" across time steps), well-tuned behavior cloning might be sufficient and more practical. The paper also clarifies that the perceived benefits of online IL are more nuanced and might be limited to specific scenarios, such as policies without parameter sharing or when dealing with complex representational challenges. For a non-specialist, the takeaway is that simpler, offline imitation learning methods can be much more powerful than previously assumed, potentially making it easier to train AI systems by learning from demonstrations.
