Learning to Open and Traverse Doors with a Legged Manipulator

Mike Zhang, Yuntao Ma, Takahiro Miki, Marco Hutter
The Brief

This research presents a robot that can learn to open and walk through doors.

The core innovation is a single "policy," which is like a set of learned instructions for the robot's legs and arm, that can figure out whether to push or pull a door and then execute the action. This is achieved through a "teacher-student approach" in a simulated environment, where a more experienced simulated agent (the teacher) guides a learning agent (the student) to develop robust behaviors. Crucially, the robot estimates important door properties, like how much force is needed, on the fly during the task, without needing to be told beforehand.

This is exciting because it brings us closer to robots that can navigate complex, human-designed environments, making them more useful for tasks like delivering goods or assisting people in their homes.

Background

Imagine a robot that can navigate complex environments like a warehouse or a home. To truly be useful in such places, it needs to be able to do more than just move around; it needs to interact with its surroundings. A fundamental interaction in human-designed spaces is opening and closing doors. This seemingly simple task is surprisingly difficult for robots. Doors come in many varieties: some push open, others pull; they might have different weights, stiffnesses, or closing mechanisms. A robot needs to figure out how to manipulate the door handle, determine which way the door opens, and then navigate its own body through the opening, all while dealing with the physical forces involved. This research tackles this challenge, aiming to give robots the ability to open and pass through doors autonomously, making them more capable of operating in human-centric environments.

What they did

The core of this work is a sophisticated computer program, called a "control policy," designed for a legged robot with an arm (specifically, the ANYmal robot). This policy learns how to open and traverse doors. Instead of being explicitly programmed with rules for every possible door, it learns through a process called "reinforcement learning" (RL) in a simulated environment. Think of RL as a trial-and-error learning process where the robot is rewarded for successful actions and penalized for failures.

To make this learning robust and adaptable, they used a "teacher-student" approach. A "teacher" policy, trained with access to extra information (called "privileged information") only available in simulation (like the exact door properties), learns a highly effective way to open doors. Then, a "student" policy is trained to mimic the teacher's actions. Crucially, the student policy only has access to the information a real robot would have during operation: its own body's state (like joint positions and velocities) and basic information about the door's location. A key innovation is that this single student policy is trained to handle both "push" and "pull" doors without being told beforehand which type it is. It learns to infer the door's opening direction through interaction. The student policy also learns to estimate important door properties, like its stiffness or how much it needs to be turned, which helps it adapt on the fly.

What they found

The results are impressive. When deployed on the real ANYmal robot, the learned policy achieved a success rate of 95.0% in opening and traversing through a spring-loaded door over repeated trials. The policy demonstrated the ability to handle various door types (push and pull, left and right swings) and adapt to different door properties like dimensions and dynamics. The researchers showed that the policy's internal "belief" about the door type (push or pull) changed over time as it interacted with the door, and these beliefs directly influenced its actions. For example, if it initially thought a door was a pull door but then needed to push it, its actions would shift accordingly. They also found that the recurrent nature of the student policy (its ability to remember past states) was crucial for its success, especially when dealing with noisy sensor data.

Why it matters

This research represents a significant step towards robots that can operate more autonomously in human environments. The ability to open doors is a fundamental requirement for robots to access many spaces, from homes and offices to industrial facilities. By developing a single, adaptable policy that doesn't need prior knowledge about door types, this work makes robots more versatile and less reliant on pre-programmed routines or human intervention. The 95.0% success rate in real-world tests is a strong indicator of the robustness of their approach.

However, there are limitations. The policy sometimes failed if it didn't turn the handle enough to unlock the door, as it lacked direct feedback on the handle's state. Also, the performance could be degraded by unmodeled noise from tracking systems like AprilTags, and by slight differences in door geometry between simulation and reality. Future work aims to address these by incorporating more sophisticated sensing (like force sensors) and expanding the range of door handles the robot can interact with. Ultimately, this work demonstrates a powerful learning-based approach to a complex robotic manipulation task, paving the way for more capable and independent robots.
