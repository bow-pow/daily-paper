Khintchine dichotomy for self-similar measures

Timothée Bénard, Weikun He, Han Zhang
The Brief

This research establishes a fundamental mathematical property for a broad class of "fractal" probability distributions on the number line.

The core idea is to prove a version of Khintchine's theorem, a classic result about how often certain numbers appear in a specific mathematical expansion, but applied to distributions that are "self-similar." Self-similar means these distributions have a repeating pattern at different scales, like the intricate, repeating patterns found in fractals. The proof involves understanding the behavior of a specific type of random process, akin to a random walk on a geometric space, which exhibits a kind of even spreading.

This work is interesting because it solves a long-standing problem about the distribution of numbers related to the famous Cantor set, a classic example of a fractal, and offers a new perspective on how complex, repeating mathematical structures behave.

Background

Imagine you're trying to understand how well you can approximate irrational numbers with fractions. For example, how close can a fraction *p/q* get to the number pi (π)? This is a classic problem in number theory. Khintchine's theorem provides a general answer for "most" irrational numbers. It says that if you're looking for fractions *p/q* such that the difference |*q*π - *p*| is smaller than some shrinking value ψ(*q*) (where ψ is a "well-behaved" function of *q*), then whether you find infinitely many such fractions depends precisely on whether the sum of all possible values of ψ(*q*) diverges or converges. If the sum diverges, almost all irrational numbers are "very well" approximable. If it converges, they are "not so well" approximable.

Now, mathematicians are interested in what happens when you restrict your attention to numbers that have a special, "fractal" structure. A prime example is the set of numbers between 0 and 1 that can be represented in base 3 using only the digits 0 and 2 (this is related to the famous Cantor set). These numbers are called "self-similar measures" because they exhibit a kind of repeating pattern at different scales. The question is: does Khintchine's theorem still hold for these "fractal" numbers? Specifically, for numbers within this fractal set, can we say something similar about how well they can be approximated by fractions? This is the problem the paper tackles, extending Khintchine's theorem to a broad class of self-similar measures.

What they did

The core of the paper is to prove an analogue of Khintchine's theorem for self-similar measures. The strategy involves a sophisticated connection between number theory and the study of dynamical systems, specifically "homogeneous dynamics." They translate the problem of approximating numbers with fractions into the language of random walks on a specific mathematical space called $\text{SL}_{2}(\mathbb{R})/\text{SL}_{2}(\mathbb{Z})$. This space can be thought of as a collection of "horocycles" (like arcs of circles) within a hyperbolic plane.

The paper establishes two key results. First,

Theorem B

(and its more general version

Theorem B'

) shows that a self-similar measure, when viewed on these horocycles and expanded by a certain flow, "equidistributes." This means the measure spreads out evenly over the space, with a quantifiable rate of convergence. This is a result of independent interest in the field of dynamical systems and relates to understanding the behavior of chaotic systems. Second,

Theorem C

(and its general version

Theorem C'

) proves an "effective equidistribution" for random walks on this space. This means that as the random walk progresses, its distribution becomes uniform across the space at a predictable speed.

The proof of these theorems is intricate. It involves showing that these random walks "gain dimension" over time (meaning their distribution becomes more spread out and complex), a process they call "dimensional bootstrap." This dimensional gain is then used to prove the equidistribution results. Finally, these equidistribution theorems are used to prove the main Khintchine dichotomy for self-similar measures.

What they found

The main result is

Theorem A

, which states that for any self-similar probability measure $\sigma$ on the real line and any non-increasing approximation function $\psi$, the set of $\psi$-approximable points within the support of $\sigma$ has measure 0 if the sum $\sum_{q \in \mathbb{N}} \psi(q)$ converges, and measure 1 if the sum diverges. This is precisely the Khintchine dichotomy, now proven for *all* self-similar measures.

A significant specific finding is that this result is new and answers an old question of Mahler concerning the Hausdorff measure on the middle-thirds Cantor set. The paper also provides quantitative estimates on the number of rational approximations in the divergent case.

The paper also establishes

Theorem B

(and

B'

) concerning the effective equidistribution of self-similar measures under an expanding flow on $\text{SL}_{2}(\mathbb{R})/\text{SL}_{2}(\mathbb{Z})$. This theorem states that for a self-similar measure $\sigma$, the measure of the form $a(t)u(s)x d\sigma(s)$ equidistributes towards the Haar measure on $\text{SL}_{2}(\mathbb{R})/\text{SL}_{2}(\mathbb{Z})$ at a rate of $t^{-c}$ for some constant $c > 0$. Additionally,

Theorem C

(and

C'

) shows that random walks on $\text{SL}_{2}(\mathbb{R})/\text{SL}_{2}(\mathbb{Z})$ driven by certain expanding matrices equidistribute at a rate of $e^{-cn}$ for some $c > 0$.

Why it matters

This work provides a complete generalization of Khintchine's theorem to a very broad class of fractal measures, specifically self-similar measures. This is a significant achievement because previous results often required strong assumptions on the fractal structure (e.g., contractive transformations, specific types of Cantor sets). The fact that the theorem now holds for *all* self-similar measures, including the historically important middle-thirds Cantor set, is a major step forward.

The paper's approach, bridging number theory and homogeneous dynamics, is powerful. The effective equidistribution results (Theorems B and C) are themselves important contributions to the study of dynamical systems, offering quantitative understanding of how measures spread out. For a non-specialist, the takeaway is that the fundamental principles governing how well numbers can be approximated by fractions, as described by Khintchine's theorem, remarkably extend to numbers with complex fractal structures. This suggests a deep underlying unity between number theoretic properties and geometric/dynamical properties of these sets. The work also provides a concrete answer to a long-standing question posed by the mathematician Kurt Mahler.
