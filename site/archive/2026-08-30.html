Information geometry approach to quantum stochastic thermodynamics

Laetitia P. Bettmann, John Goold
The Brief

This research explores how geometric concepts, usually applied to maps and curves, can describe the behavior of tiny quantum systems undergoing heat and work exchanges.

The core idea is that the "shape" of a quantum system's possible states, measured by something called the quantum Fisher information, can be broken down into parts. One part, the "incoherent" part, behaves much like it does in classical systems and relates to how quickly information is lost or gained, and how thermodynamic forces change. The other part, the "coherent" contribution, is unique to quantum mechanics and arises from the system's quantum nature, like superposition. This geometric perspective allows for new ways to understand fundamental limits on how fast thermodynamic processes can occur, even for complex quantum systems.

Understanding these geometric links could lead to better designs for quantum technologies, like more efficient quantum computers or sensors, by revealing fundamental trade-offs in how they operate.

Background

Understanding how systems change over time, especially when they aren't in a stable, balanced state (like water freezing or a hot cup of coffee cooling down), is a major goal in physics. This area, called stochastic thermodynamics, looks at the energy and entropy (a measure of disorder) involved in these processes. Recently, scientists have noticed that the way a system's probabilities change over time can be described using tools from geometry, specifically "information geometry." This field treats probability distributions as points in a geometric space, and measures distances and curvatures between them. The challenge has been to translate these powerful geometric insights from classical systems to the quantum world, where things like superposition and entanglement add complexity.

What they did

This research builds a bridge between information geometry and quantum thermodynamics. They start by recognizing that in quantum mechanics, there isn't just one way to measure the "distance" or "information content" between quantum states; there's a whole family of measures, called quantum Fisher Information (QFI) metrics. The key insight is that any of these QFIs can be broken down into two parts: an "incoherent" part, which is independent of the specific metric chosen and relates to the probabilities of outcomes, and a "coherent" part, which depends on the metric and captures the quantum nature of the state (like superposition).

They then show that this incoherent part of the QFI is directly linked to "entropic acceleration" – how quickly the rate of entropy change is itself changing. For specific types of quantum evolution (those described by GKSL dynamics with local detailed balance), they demonstrate that this incoherent component can be expressed in terms of changes in "generalized thermodynamic forces" (which drive transitions between states) and the "entropic flow" (how entropy moves between the system and its environment). This mirrors findings from classical systems.

Furthermore, they refine a classical "uncertainty relation" that connects how "wiggly" a system's path is through its state space (geometric uncertainty) with how much information is gained over time. They show this relation also holds for quantum systems and is even tighter than previously thought. They also generalize a classical bound on how fast entropy can change in non-equilibrium processes, adding a new, non-negative quantum term that accounts for the "geometric action" arising from coherent dynamics. Finally, they apply this entire framework to a recently observed quantum phenomenon called the "quantum thermodynamic Mpemba effect," which describes situations where a hotter system might cool down faster than a colder one under certain conditions.

What they found

The core findings are: 1.

Decomposition of QFI

Any quantum Fisher Information (QFI) can be split into a metric-independent "incoherent" part and a metric-dependent "coherent" part. 2.

Link to Thermodynamics

The incoherent part of the QFI is directly related to entropic acceleration. For specific quantum dynamics, it connects to the rate of change of generalized thermodynamic forces and entropic flow, paralleling classical results. 3.

Tighter Uncertainty Relation

A classical uncertainty relation between geometric path uncertainty and the rate of information change is shown to hold for quantum systems and is tightened by a factor of two. For example, in the context of the Mpemba effect, they show that a state that evolves purely incoherently saturates this bound. 4.

Generalized Entropy Bound

A classical bound on the entropy rate for far-from-equilibrium processes is extended to quantum systems by adding a non-negative "quantum contribution" stemming from coherent dynamics. This bound is tightest for the Symmetric Logarithmic Derivative (SLD) QFI. 5.

Mpemba Effect Explained

The quantum thermodynamic Mpemba effect can be understood using this information-geometric framework, specifically by analyzing geometric quantities like the "ratio of completion" and deviations from geodesic paths. They show that a state with higher initial non-equilibrium free energy (which relaxes faster) exhibits a higher ratio of completion.

Why it matters

This work provides a powerful and unified information-geometric language to describe quantum thermodynamic processes. By decomposing the QFI, it clarifies how quantum coherence contributes to thermodynamic behavior, separating it from the more classical aspects related to probability distributions. The tighter uncertainty relations and generalized bounds offer new constraints and insights into the efficiency and speed of quantum processes, especially far from equilibrium. The successful application to the quantum Mpemba effect demonstrates the framework's ability to capture subtle and counterintuitive thermodynamic phenomena. This research opens doors for further exploration of complex quantum dynamics, potentially leading to better control over quantum systems for applications in quantum computing, thermodynamics, and beyond. It highlights that geometric properties of quantum states are not just abstract mathematical concepts but have direct physical consequences for how systems evolve and dissipate energy.
