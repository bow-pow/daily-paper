Constraining Light QCD Axions with Isolated Neutron Star Cooling

Antonio Gómez-Bañón, Kai Bartnick, Konstantin Springmann, José A. Pons
The Brief

This research uses the way neutron stars cool down to search for a hypothetical particle called a QCD axion.

The core idea is that if these light QCD axions exist, they can interact with matter inside neutron stars, effectively making the particles that make up the star (nucleons) lighter. This change in nucleon mass would alter how heat escapes from the neutron star, specifically by making the outer layer that traps heat (the heat-blanketing envelope) thinner or non-existent. By observing neutron stars that appear to be cooling faster than expected, scientists can rule out certain possibilities for the axion's properties, like its mass. This analysis doesn't assume the axion is dark matter.

This is interesting because it connects the extreme physics of neutron stars to fundamental questions about particle physics and the possibility of new, undiscovered particles influencing the universe.

Background

Physicists are always looking for new particles that might explain some of the universe's mysteries, like dark matter. One such hypothetical particle is the "QCD axion." While axions are theorized to exist, their exact properties, like their mass, are not precisely known. This uncertainty means there's a vast range of possibilities for what axions could be like. The problem this research tackles is how to narrow down these possibilities and rule out certain types of axions that don't fit with what we observe in the universe. Specifically, they want to see if a particular kind of "light" QCD axion can be ruled out by looking at neutron stars.

What they did

The research focuses on the behavior of neutron stars, which are incredibly dense remnants of collapsed stars. Neutron stars cool down over time, and their cooling rate is a sensitive probe of the physics happening inside them. The core idea here is that certain light QCD axions, under the extreme conditions inside a neutron star, can interact with the matter in a way that fundamentally changes the star's structure.

Here's how it works: *

Axion Interaction

The paper describes how a QCD axion can interact with the particles that make up atomic nuclei (protons and neutrons). This interaction, especially at the high densities found in neutron stars, can cause the axion field to "source" itself, meaning it starts to exist and influence the surrounding matter. *

New Ground State of Matter

When this sourced axion field is present, it can reduce the "effective mass" of nucleons. Think of effective mass as how "heavy" a particle behaves in a particular environment. By reducing this, the axion can create a new, more stable state of matter within the neutron star. This is called a "new ground state" (NGS). *

Structural Change

The presence of this NGS has a dramatic effect on the outer layers of the neutron star, known as the "heat-blanketing envelope." This envelope is crucial for regulating how heat escapes from the star's core to its surface. The axion-induced NGS can make this envelope much thinner, or even prevent it from forming altogether. *

Altered Cooling

A thinner or absent envelope means heat can escape much more easily and quickly from the neutron star's core. This leads to an anomalously fast cooling rate compared to what standard models predict. *

Observational Comparison

The researchers then compare the predicted cooling rates of neutron stars with these axion-induced structural changes to the observed cooling rates of actual isolated neutron stars. By looking at data from these stars, they can identify if any observed cooling patterns are too fast to be explained by normal physics, suggesting the presence of such axions. They specifically look at the luminosity (brightness) of neutron stars at around 100,000 years old, as this is when photon emission from the surface becomes dominant and is sensitive to envelope properties.

What they found

The study found that certain types of light QCD axions, specifically those with a parameter called 'ϵ' less than or equal to about 0.1 and a decay constant 'fa' below approximately 10¹⁵ GeV, would cause neutron stars to cool much faster than observed. This fast cooling is inconsistent with the observed luminosities of many isolated neutron stars, particularly a group known as the "magnificent seven," which are observed to be around 10⁶ years old and have luminosities between 10³¹ and 10³³ erg/s. The research places constraints on the axion parameter space, ruling out regions where this anomalous fast cooling would be expected. For instance, they found that if the effective surface gravity (including the axion's contribution) is significantly larger than about 50 times the normal surface gravity (specifically, (g + ga)¹⁴ > 50), the cooling is too fast to match observations. This translates to excluding axion masses (ma) above a certain threshold, depending on the axion decay constant (fa).

Why it matters

This research provides a novel way to search for and constrain hypothetical particles like QCD axions, not by directly detecting them, but by observing their indirect effects on astrophysical objects like neutron stars. The key takeaway is that the cooling behavior of neutron stars can act as a powerful "detector" for new physics. By ruling out specific axion properties, this work narrows down the possibilities for what axions could be, guiding future theoretical and experimental searches. It's important to note that this analysis doesn't require the axion to be the elusive dark matter particle; it focuses solely on its potential impact on neutron star physics. The study highlights the model-dependent nature of these constraints, acknowledging that different theoretical frameworks for axions might evade these specific limits, but it confidently excludes any theory that predicts a similar axion-induced structural change leading to fast neutron star cooling.
