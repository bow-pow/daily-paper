Quantum thermal state preparation for near-term quantum processors

Jerome Lloyd, Dmitry A. Abanin
The Brief

This research presents a practical method for quantum computers to create a specific type of quantum state called a "thermal state," which is crucial for simulating how physical systems behave at different temperatures.

The core idea involves cleverly controlling the interaction between the system being studied and a "bath" – essentially a reservoir of energy. By carefully "resetting" this bath and modulating their connection, the algorithm creates a process that approximates a fundamental physical principle called detailed balance. This ensures the final state closely resembles the true thermal state, with the accuracy depending on how strongly the system and bath are coupled. Extensive simulations on models like the 2D Quantum Ising model confirm this approach works well, even near critical temperature points.

This development is exciting because it offers a way to use today's and near-future quantum computers to explore complex quantum phenomena at realistic temperatures, opening doors for discoveries in materials science, drug discovery, and even solving difficult optimization problems.

Background

Imagine you want to understand how a material behaves at a certain temperature. In physics, this behavior is often described by a "thermal state," which is like a statistical snapshot of all the tiny particles in the material. Specifically, we're interested in the Gibbs state, which tells us the probability of finding the system in any particular configuration of its particles at a given temperature. The challenge is that preparing this exact thermal state on a quantum computer is incredibly difficult. It's like trying to perfectly replicate the chaotic jiggling of molecules in a hot cup of coffee by controlling individual atoms – a monumental task. This difficulty arises from two main issues: the need for a very large "environment" (or "bath") for the system to interact with to dissipate energy, and the fundamental limits imposed by quantum mechanics on how quickly we can distinguish between different energy levels.

What they did

This research proposes a clever new algorithm to prepare these thermal states on near-term quantum computers. The core idea is to couple the system we're interested in (the "system") to a small, controllable set of auxiliary quantum bits (the "bath"). This coupling isn't constant; it's "modulated" or changed over time using a specific "filter function." Think of it like carefully tuning a radio dial to slowly guide the system towards its thermal state.

The algorithm has three main steps, repeated many times: 1.

Initialize the bath

The auxiliary qubits are reset to a simple, known state (the $|0\rangle$ state). 2.

Joint Unitary Evolution

The system and bath qubits interact via a carefully designed quantum operation. This operation involves evolving the system under its own natural rules (its "Hamiltonian") and simultaneously coupling it to the bath. The strength of this coupling is modulated over time by a "filter function." This modulation is key to satisfying a principle called "quantum detailed balance," which is crucial for reaching the correct thermal state. 3.

Bath Reset

After the interaction, the bath qubits are reset again.

A key innovation is the addition of a "randomization step." This involves evolving the system for a short, random amount of time. The researchers found this step helps to suppress unwanted "coherences" – a quantum phenomenon where a system can exist in multiple states simultaneously – which can prevent accurate thermal state preparation.

What they found

The algorithm's success is measured by how closely the prepared state matches the ideal Gibbs state. The researchers showed, both theoretically and through extensive numerical simulations, that the error in preparing the thermal state scales as the square of the system-bath coupling strength ($\theta^2$). This means that even with a small, but non-zero, coupling, the prepared state is a good approximation of the true thermal state.

They tested this on two examples: *

Single spin cooling

For a single quantum bit, they showed that the error in populations (how likely the system is to be in each energy level) and coherences (off-diagonal elements in the quantum state) both decrease as $\theta^2$. They also demonstrated that the randomization step effectively suppresses problematic resonances that can occur in the unrandomized version. *

2D Quantum Ising Model

This is a more complex, many-body system that exhibits a phase transition. The algorithm successfully prepared thermal states across the entire finite-temperature phase diagram, including near the critical point of the phase transition. They observed excellent agreement between the simulated steady-state energy and heat capacity and the exact thermal values. For instance, the relative error in energy decreased with decreasing coupling strength $\theta$. They also showed that even for complex properties like transverse magnetization and mutual information (a measure of correlation), the prepared state closely matched the thermal state.

Why it matters

This work offers a practical pathway for quantum computers to simulate complex quantum systems at finite temperatures. This is crucial for advancing fields like condensed matter physics, quantum chemistry, and even certain types of classical optimization problems. The algorithm's simplicity and reliance on components accessible to current and near-term quantum hardware (like controllable system-bath coupling and fast bath resetting) make it particularly promising.

The key takeaway is that by carefully engineering the interaction between a quantum system and a small, resettable bath, and by modulating this interaction over time, it's possible to efficiently guide the system towards its thermal equilibrium state. The theoretical guarantee that errors scale with $\theta^2$ provides confidence that this method can achieve high accuracy by simply reducing the strength of the system-bath coupling. While challenges remain, particularly in understanding the "mixing time" (how long it takes to reach equilibrium) in certain complex systems, this research presents a significant step forward for quantum simulation at finite temperatures.
