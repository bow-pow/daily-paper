Discover-then-Name: Task-Agnostic Concept Bottlenecks via Automated Concept Discovery

Sukrut Rao, Sweta Mahajan, Moritz Böhle, Bernt Schiele
The Brief

This research introduces a new way to understand how complex AI models make decisions, making them more transparent.

The core idea is to automatically find the "concepts" an AI has learned, rather than forcing it to use concepts we pre-define. They use a technique called sparse autoencoders to uncover these hidden concepts within the AI's internal workings. Once discovered, these concepts are given human-understandable names, and then used for classification tasks. This approach is efficient because it doesn't need to know the specific task beforehand and relies on concepts the AI already grasps.

This work is interesting because it offers a path towards AI systems that are not only powerful but also explainable, allowing us to better trust and debug them, which is crucial as AI becomes more integrated into our lives.

Background

Imagine you're trying to understand how a complex computer program, like one that recognizes images, makes its decisions. These programs, often called "deep neural networks," are incredibly powerful but act like black boxes. We can see what goes in (an image) and what comes out (a label, like "cat" or "dog"), but the internal workings are mysterious. Researchers have tried to peek inside using methods that highlight which parts of the image were most important for the decision. However, these methods can sometimes be misleading. Another approach is to build "interpretable models" that are designed to be understandable from the start. A popular type of interpretable model is a "Concept Bottleneck Model" (CBM). The idea behind CBMs is to first translate an image into a set of human-understandable concepts (like "has fur," "is brown," "has pointy ears") and then use these concepts to make the final classification. The challenge with traditional CBMs is that you first need to decide what concepts are important for a given task, and then train the model to recognize them. But what if the model already "knows" about certain concepts, even if we didn't explicitly tell it to look for them? And what if those pre-defined concepts aren't actually detectable by the model? This is the problem this research addresses: how to build interpretable models without having to guess the right concepts beforehand.

What they did

This research proposes a new way to build these interpretable models, called Discover-then-Name CBM (DN-CBM). Instead of humans deciding on the concepts first, DN-CBM lets the computer discover concepts that a powerful pre-trained vision-language model called CLIP already learns. CLIP is special because it can understand both images and text, and it learns to connect them.

The process has three main steps: 1.

Concept Discovery

They use a technique called a "sparse autoencoder" (SAE). Think of an autoencoder as a system that tries to compress information and then reconstruct it. An SAE does this by learning a set of "dictionary vectors" (like fundamental building blocks) and then representing the input (in this case, the internal features of CLIP's image processing) as a very sparse combination of these vectors. "Sparse" means only a few of these building blocks are used at any time. This sparsity helps make the individual building blocks more focused on a single idea or "concept." They train this SAE on a large dataset of images (CC3M) without needing any labels, just to learn how to reconstruct CLIP's image features. 2.

Concept Naming

Once the SAE has discovered these concept-representing building blocks (the dictionary vectors), the next step is to give them human-readable names. They leverage CLIP's ability to understand text. For each discovered concept's dictionary vector, they find the word from a large vocabulary (like the 20,000 most common English words) whose text embedding (CLIP's numerical representation of the word) is most similar to the concept's dictionary vector. This similarity is measured using "cosine similarity," a common way to compare vector directions. This automatically assigns a name to each discovered concept. 3.

Concept Bottleneck Model Construction

With the discovered and named concepts, they create the actual interpretable classifier. They take the "concept activations" (how strongly each discovered concept is present in an image) and train a simple linear classifier on top of them. This linear classifier learns to combine the strengths of these named concepts to make the final prediction for a given task (e.g., classifying an image as "cat" or "dog"). Crucially, this concept discovery and naming step is done *once* and is "task-agnostic," meaning it doesn't depend on the specific classification task you'll eventually want to solve. The same set of discovered concepts can then be used for many different classification tasks.

What they found

The research demonstrates that DN-CBM is effective in several ways. First, the discovered concepts are semantically meaningful and can be accurately named. For example, they show that concepts discovered from one dataset (CC3M) can be applied to images from completely different datasets (like ImageNet, CIFAR10, CIFAR100, and Places365) and still activate images consistent with the assigned name (e.g., a "striped" concept activates images with stripes across all datasets). A user study confirmed that their discovered concepts are significantly more semantically consistent and accurately named compared to a baseline method that dissects individual neurons. For instance, in one comparison, their method achieved a higher "consistency" score (rated 3.9 out of 5) compared to the baseline (rated 2.7 out of 5) for concepts with high alignment to their names.

Second, the DN-CBM models built using these discovered concepts are performant classifiers. When tested on various datasets like ImageNet and Places365, their DN-CBM models achieved competitive or even superior accuracy compared to existing "label-free" concept bottleneck methods that are specifically optimized for each task. For example, on the Places365 dataset using a CLIP ResNet-50 backbone, their method achieved 53.5% accuracy, outperforming the baseline CDM method at 52.7%.

Third, the interpretability of the models is strong. They show examples where the model's decisions are explained by intuitive and relevant concepts. For instance, when classifying a "glacier," the model might use concepts like "alps," "snowy," and "everest." They also demonstrate that the model can use concepts to explain decisions in a nuanced way, with different concepts being important for similar classes depending on the specific image content. Finally, they show that these concept bottlenecks can be used for interventions, like selectively keeping or removing concepts to improve or degrade model performance on specific subgroups of data, demonstrating the practical utility of the discovered concepts.

Why it matters

This work offers a significant step towards making AI models more transparent and trustworthy. By automating the discovery and naming of concepts, DN-CBM removes the need for human experts to guess which concepts are relevant for a given task. This makes the process of building interpretable models more efficient and less prone to human bias or oversight. The fact that the concept discovery is "task-agnostic" means that a single set of learned concepts can be reused across many different classification problems, which is a more scalable and robust approach.

For a non-specialist, the key takeaway is that researchers are developing ways to build AI systems that can explain their reasoning in terms of concepts we can understand, rather than just being opaque "black boxes." This is crucial for deploying AI in sensitive areas like healthcare, finance, or autonomous driving, where understanding *why* a decision was made is as important as the decision itself. While the research shows great promise, it also points to future directions. The authors suggest that training on even larger datasets and with more concept dimensions could lead to even richer and more fine-grained concepts, potentially improving performance on highly specialized tasks.
