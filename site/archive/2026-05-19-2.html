The bound origin of low-mass stellar binaries

A. Generozov, S. S. R. Offner, K. M. Kratter, H. B. Perets, D. Guszejnov, M. Y. Grudi\'c
The Brief

This research explores how most stars, unlike our Sun, end up in pairs or groups, a process that's been hard to observe directly.

By running detailed computer simulations of star formation in conditions similar to our Milky Way, the study reveals that a large majority of stellar pairs, around 70-80%, are already gravitationally bound – meaning they are held together by their mutual gravity – right from the moment the second star in the pair forms. These bound pairs then evolve and grow together, which has significant consequences for any planets that might form around them and their chemical makeup. Interestingly, about half of these initial pairs are broken apart by the end of the star formation period, meaning many stars that end up alone actually started out in a multiple system.

This finding is fascinating because it suggests that forming in groups is the primary way stars come into being, impacting our understanding of how stars are distributed and how planetary systems form.

Background

Most stars in our galaxy, unlike our solitary Sun, are part of systems with two or more stars. Understanding how these stellar companions come together and form stable pairs is a fundamental question in astronomy. This is challenging because the earliest moments of star formation, when these systems are born, are hidden from direct observation. This research aims to shed light on this crucial, early stage of stellar life.

What they did

To investigate how binary stars form, researchers used sophisticated computer simulations. These simulations model the formation of star clusters, which are groups of stars born together, under conditions similar to those in our Milky Way. The simulations incorporate a wide range of physical processes that are known to be important during star formation, including gravity, turbulence (chaotic gas motion), magnetic fields, and "stellar feedback" (energy and material ejected by young stars, like radiation and outflows).

The simulations track the formation of stars from dense clouds of gas and dust. When two stars form close to each other, the researchers analyze whether they are gravitationally bound – meaning their mutual gravity is strong enough to keep them together. A key aspect of their method is accounting for the "gas halo" – the substantial amount of gas surrounding each newly formed star. This gas can significantly influence the gravitational pull between stars, especially in the early stages. They identified binary systems by looking for pairs of stars that are gravitationally bound, considering both the stars' masses and the mass of their surrounding gas. They then followed these systems over time to see if they remained bound or were disrupted.

What they found

The simulations reveal a surprising and significant finding: approximately 70-80% of binary star systems are already gravitationally bound from the very moment the second star in the pair forms. This suggests that many binaries are not formed through later chance encounters but are "born bound." This "bound from birth" fraction varies with the mass of the stars, being higher for less massive stars (around 80%) and lower for more massive stars (around 25%). While massive stars tend to form in multiple systems, they are also more prone to disruptive interactions.

However, the star-forming environment is dynamic. About half of these initially bound binaries are disrupted by the end of the star-formation epoch (roughly the first 10 million years of a star's life). This disruption often occurs due to gravitational interactions with other stars in the crowded cluster environment. Consequently, about 40% of stars that end up as single stars actually started their lives as part of a binary or higher-order multiple system. Overall, the simulations indicate that formation in multiple systems is the dominant mode of star formation, accounting for at least 57% of all stars.

Why it matters

This research fundamentally changes our understanding of how most stars in the universe come to be paired. The finding that a large majority of binaries are bound from birth has important implications for several areas of astrophysics. For instance, the early presence of a binary companion can significantly affect the formation and evolution of planetary systems around those stars, potentially truncating protoplanetary disks. It also impacts our understanding of stellar evolution, as binary companions can influence how stars age and die.

Furthermore, the fact that many single stars have a multiple origin means that their properties might be subtly different from stars that were always single. This could have implications for the "stellar initial mass function" (IMF), which describes the distribution of stellar masses at birth. While the simulations show this effect is modest, it suggests that the history of a star within a multiple system could leave a detectable imprint. The study highlights the complexity of star formation, emphasizing that stars don't just form in isolation but often emerge from a chaotic, interactive environment where companionship is the norm.
