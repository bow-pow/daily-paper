Observation of first- and second-order dissipative phase transitions in a two-photon driven Kerr resonator

Guillaume Beaulieu, Fabrizio Minganti, Simone Frasca, Vincenzo Savona, Simone Felicetti, Roberto Di Candia et al.
The Brief

This research experimentally demonstrates and theoretically explains two distinct ways a quantum system can change its fundamental behavior as you tweak its parameters, like turning a knob.

The core finding is observing "dissipative phase transitions" in a special type of superconducting circuit called a Kerr resonator. These transitions, where the system's stable state changes, can be either smooth (second-order) or abrupt with a distinct "jump" (first-order). The researchers show that in their setup, driven by two light particles at once, they can precisely control and observe both types of transitions, seeing phenomena like squeezing of quantum noise below the usual limits and the coexistence of different system states. They also found that as they approach a large-scale system, the time it takes for these transitions to happen can become incredibly long, spanning vast ranges.

This work is interesting because it shows how we can engineer and control quantum criticality, the tendency of systems to exhibit these sharp changes, in practical superconducting circuits, which could be crucial for building more powerful quantum computers and sensors.

Background

Imagine a system that's constantly being nudged and influenced by its surroundings, like a tiny quantum object interacting with the environment. In such "open quantum systems," things don't just settle into a single, predictable state. Instead, they can exhibit fascinating "phase transitions," similar to how water can turn into ice or steam. These are called "dissipative phase transitions" (DPTs) because they happen due to the system's interaction with its environment (dissipation) and the driving forces acting on it. Scientists are particularly interested in understanding these transitions because they can reveal fundamental physics and potentially be harnessed for new quantum technologies. The challenge has been to experimentally observe and control different types of these transitions, especially those that are more complex to manifest.

What they did

This research focuses on a specific type of quantum system: a superconducting circuit called a Kerr resonator. Think of it as a tiny, artificial atom that can hold "quanta" of energy, which we call photons. The "Kerr" part means its behavior changes depending on how many photons are already inside – it's nonlinear. What's novel here is how they "drive" this resonator. Instead of a simple push (single-photon drive), they use a "two-photon drive," which is like a synchronized push and pull, making the system behave in more complex ways.

They used this two-photon driven Kerr resonator to explore two specific kinds of DPTs: first-order and second-order. The order of a phase transition is determined by how smoothly the system's properties change. A second-order transition is more gradual, like a continuous change in temperature. A first-order transition is more abrupt, like water suddenly freezing.

To study these transitions, they carefully controlled a parameter called "detuning" – essentially, how closely the driving frequency matches the resonator's natural frequency. By sweeping this detuning, they observed how the system's steady state (its long-term behavior) changed. They also looked at the "dynamics," meaning how the system moved between states, by monitoring it over time using techniques like "quantum trajectories." This involves observing the system's evolution along many individual "paths" or histories, giving a detailed picture of its behavior. They employed advanced theoretical tools, specifically "Liouvillian spectral theory," to analyze the rates at which these transitions occurred and how they changed as they approached a theoretical "thermodynamic limit" (where the system effectively becomes very large).

What they found

The researchers successfully observed both first- and second-order DPTs in their two-photon driven Kerr resonator. At the second-order critical point, they observed a quantum effect called "squeezing below vacuum." This means the uncertainty in certain properties of the photons became even smaller than the fundamental quantum limit, indicating a highly quantum state. They also witnessed "spontaneous symmetry breaking," where the system, which initially had a balanced symmetry, settled into one of two asymmetrical states.

For the first-order DPT, they found clear evidence of "hysteresis cycles." This means that if you sweep the control parameter (detuning) up and then down, the system follows different paths, creating a loop. This is a hallmark of first-order transitions and indicates the presence of "metastable states" – temporary states that the system can get stuck in before eventually settling into its true steady state. They also observed the "coexistence of two phases" with different photon numbers, a key characteristic of first-order transitions.

Crucially, they measured the "timescales" associated with these transitions, which represent how long it takes for the system to react or settle. They found that as they scaled the system towards the thermodynamic limit, these timescales spanned an astonishing five orders of magnitude, meaning some processes were incredibly fast and others incredibly slow. Their experimental results closely matched predictions from their theoretical models.

Why it matters

This work is significant because it's the first comprehensive experimental and theoretical study of *both* first- and second-order dissipative phase transitions in a *two-photon* driven system. Previously, second-order transitions were mostly theoretical, and first-order ones were observed in simpler, single-photon driven systems. This research demonstrates a powerful new platform – the two-photon driven Kerr resonator – for exploring and controlling quantum criticality.

The ability to engineer and observe these critical phenomena is important for advancing quantum information applications. For instance, the unique properties of second-order DPTs could be used to improve quantum computing by making quantum information more robust against errors. The precise control over criticality demonstrated here also opens doors for developing highly sensitive quantum sensors. In essence, this research shows that by carefully designing and controlling quantum systems, we can tap into the fundamental principles of phase transitions for practical technological advancements.
