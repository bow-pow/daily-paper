Bounds on the bubble wall velocity

Wen-Yuan Ai, Benoit Laurent, Jorinde van de Vis
The Brief

This research provides a way to estimate the speed of expanding "bubbles" during a rapid change in the universe's fundamental state, without needing all the complex details of particle interactions.

The core idea is to establish limits for this speed by considering two extreme scenarios: one where particles are perfectly in sync with their surroundings (local thermal equilibrium) and another where they travel freely (ballistic limit). Crucially, these limits are found to be independent of the precise, and often uncertain, calculations of how particles collide. By accounting for how the temperature and movement of particles change near the bubble wall, the ballistic limit is shown to also include a "drag" effect, making it a more realistic lower bound.

This work is interesting because understanding these rapid cosmic transitions is key to explaining phenomena like the origin of matter and the structure of the universe, and this method offers a more robust way to model them.

Background

Imagine the very early universe, a time of rapid change and transformation. Many theories suggest that at this time, the universe underwent "phase transitions," similar to how water freezes into ice or boils into steam. These cosmic phase transitions are thought to have involved the creation of expanding "bubbles" of a new vacuum state within the old. The speed at which the walls of these bubbles expand is crucial for many important cosmic phenomena, such as the generation of gravitational waves or the creation of matter. However, calculating this bubble wall velocity is incredibly difficult. It requires solving complex equations that describe both the fundamental field driving the transition and the behavior of particles in the surrounding plasma. A major hurdle is that our understanding of how these particles interact (their "collision terms") is often imprecise, leading to significant uncertainties in the calculated velocity. This research aims to overcome this by finding reliable upper and lower bounds for the bubble wall velocity, independent of these uncertain collision details.

What they did

The core idea is to simplify the problem by considering two extreme scenarios for the behavior of particles in the plasma as the bubble wall passes through:

1.

Local Thermal Equilibrium (LTE)

This scenario assumes that particles interact so frequently and strongly that they instantaneously "relax" to their equilibrium state as the wall passes. Think of it like a very dense crowd that quickly adjusts its arrangement. In this LTE approximation, the researchers found that the bubble wall velocity is pushed to its *upper* limit. This is because strong interactions lead to more "friction" from the plasma, slowing the wall down.

2.

Ballistic Approximation

This is the opposite extreme, where particles interact very weakly or not at all. They travel in straight lines, or "ballistically," without much scattering. This is like a sparse crowd where individuals mostly move independently. Crucially, the researchers here improved upon previous work by accounting for how the temperature and velocity of the plasma *change* across the bubble wall. This "inhomogeneous plasma" effect, previously only considered in LTE, was found to also be present in the ballistic case, creating a form of "hydrodynamic obstruction." This improved ballistic approximation was found to provide the *lower* bound on the bubble wall velocity.

By calculating the wall velocity in these two limiting cases, the researchers established a range within which the true velocity must lie, regardless of the precise, uncertain details of particle collisions. They used a "model-independent" approach, meaning their findings are generally applicable to various phase transition scenarios, and then tested their methods on a specific model.

What they found

The key finding is that the LTE approximation provides an *upper bound* on the bubble wall velocity, while the improved ballistic approximation provides a *lower bound*. This is a significant result because it constrains the possible speeds without needing to know the exact particle collision rates. For example, in one of their model comparisons, the ballistic velocity ($v_b w$) and LTE velocity ($v_{LTE} w$) could differ by as much as 0.3, meaning the true velocity could be anywhere in this range. In some specific scenarios, like when the phase transition is very strong or when particles become very massive, the ballistic and LTE velocities become very close, suggesting that the exact collision details have less impact on the wall speed in those cases. For instance, they found that when the mass difference between particles inside and outside the bubble is large, the wall velocity is minimized. They also observed that detonation solutions (a faster type of phase transition) are generally not stable in LTE but can exist in the ballistic approximation, though they often require fine-tuning of parameters.

Why it matters

This research provides a powerful tool for physicists studying the early universe and beyond-Standard Model physics. Before, estimating bubble wall velocities was plagued by uncertainties in particle collision rates. Now, researchers can use these upper and lower bounds to quickly assess the range of possible velocities for a given theoretical model. This is invaluable for initial explorations of new theories, helping to determine if they could produce observable phenomena like gravitational waves or explain cosmic puzzles like the matter-antimatter asymmetry. If the bounds are very close, it suggests that the precise details of particle interactions are less critical, simplifying further analysis. If the bounds are far apart, it highlights regions where more detailed, computationally intensive calculations are necessary to pinpoint the true velocity. Ultimately, this work offers a more robust and less uncertain way to understand a fundamental aspect of cosmic phase transitions.
