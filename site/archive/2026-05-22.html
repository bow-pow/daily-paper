Why Does Self-Distillation (Sometimes) Degrade the Reasoning Capability of LLMs?

Jeonghye Kim, Xufang Luo, Minbeom Kim, Sangmook Lee, Dohyung Kim, Jiwon Jeon et al.
The Brief

This research investigates why a technique called self-distillation, which usually makes large language models (LLMs) better, can sometimes make them worse at reasoning, especially with math problems.

The core problem identified is that self-distillation can suppress "epistemic verbalization," which is the model's way of expressing uncertainty. When a model is trained to be less uncertain, it might quickly learn to solve specific types of problems but struggles with new, unseen ones because it can't signal its doubt and adjust its approach. This leads to significant performance drops on tasks outside its direct training.

Understanding this is important because it reveals that simply teaching LLMs the right answers isn't enough for robust reasoning; they also need to learn to acknowledge what they don't know.

Here's an explainer of the research paper, structured as requested:

Background

Large Language Models (LLMs) are increasingly used for complex tasks like mathematical reasoning. A common technique to improve LLMs after their initial training is called "self-distillation." Imagine you have a student model and a "teacher" model that knows the correct answers and reasoning steps. In self-distillation, the student model learns by trying to mimic the teacher's outputs. This often leads to better performance and more concise, efficient reasoning, meaning the model gets to the answer faster with fewer words. However, this paper noticed something strange: when applying this self-distillation technique to mathematical reasoning, it sometimes made the models *worse*, even though they were still producing correct answers. This was puzzling because the goal of self-distillation is usually to improve performance.

What they did

The researchers investigated why self-distillation, which usually helps, was hurting mathematical reasoning. They focused on a phenomenon called "epistemic verbalization." This refers to how LLMs express uncertainty during their reasoning process, using phrases like "Hmm, let me double-check that" or "Perhaps there's another way." They hypothesized that self-distillation might be suppressing these expressions of uncertainty.

To test this, they conducted several experiments:

1.

Information Richness

They varied how much information the "teacher" model was given. For example, they compared giving the teacher the full, detailed solution (including the "thinking" steps) versus giving it a solution with the thinking steps removed. They found that the more information the teacher had, the more confident and less uncertain its reasoning became, and the fewer "epistemic tokens" (words indicating uncertainty) it used.

2.

Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT)

They trained a model on two sets of correct reasoning traces: one set that was "unguided" and contained many uncertainty expressions, and another set that was "solution-guided" and very concise with few uncertainty expressions. They found that training on the concise, solution-guided data significantly degraded the model's performance on math benchmarks, even though the answers were correct. This suggested that removing uncertainty expressions was harmful.

3.

On-Policy Self-Distillation

They compared different self-distillation methods (GRPO and SDPO) on mathematical reasoning tasks. They observed that when the teacher was given richer information (like the full solution), the self-distillation process led to shorter responses and a reduction in epistemic verbalization. This was associated with performance degradation, especially on problems the model hadn't seen during training (Out-of-Distribution or OOD performance).

4.

Task Coverage

They explored how the diversity of problems the model was trained on affected the outcome. They found that when the training data had limited "task coverage" (i.e., a narrow range of problem types), self-distillation's suppression of uncertainty was beneficial, leading to faster in-domain optimization. However, when the task coverage was broad and the model needed to generalize to unseen problem types, suppressing uncertainty became detrimental.

What they found

The core finding is that self-distillation, particularly when the teacher model is given very rich information (like complete solutions), tends to suppress "epistemic verbalization" – the model's expression of uncertainty. This leads to more concise reasoning traces but can significantly degrade the model's ability to reason robustly, especially on mathematical problems it hasn't encountered before.

Specifically:

*

Information Richness

More informative contexts for the teacher lead to less uncertainty expressed by the model. For instance, when the teacher was given the full solution including "think" steps, the model's responses became much shorter and contained far fewer uncertainty markers (e.g., "wait," "hmm," "perhaps"). *

Performance Impact

Training a model on concise, "solution-guided" reasoning traces (which lack uncertainty) led to performance drops of up to 40% on math benchmarks, even though the training data consisted of correct answers. *

On-Policy Self-Distillation

Standard self-distillation methods (like SDPO) often reduced epistemic verbalization and response length, but this was correlated with a substantial drop in performance on OOD math problems. For example, on the AIME24 benchmark, performance dropped by about 40%. *

Task Coverage is Key

The negative impact of suppressing uncertainty was much worse when the model was expected to generalize to a wide variety of unseen math problems. For tasks with limited diversity, suppressing uncertainty could be beneficial for in-domain performance.

Why it matters

This research highlights a critical trade-off in training LLMs for reasoning. While making models more concise and "confident" through self-distillation can be effective in some scenarios, it can inadvertently strip away a vital component of robust reasoning: the ability to express and manage uncertainty. For mathematical reasoning, where problems can be complex and require careful step-by-step deduction, knowing when the model is unsure is as important as getting the right answer.

The takeaway for anyone interested in LLMs is that simply optimizing for correct answers and shorter reasoning traces isn't enough. We need training methods that encourage models to be not just accurate, but also aware of their own limitations and uncertainties. This is crucial for building more reliable and trustworthy AI systems, especially in domains like mathematics where errors can have significant consequences. The paper suggests that future research should focus on preserving or even encouraging appropriate levels of uncertainty expression during LLM training, rather than blindly compressing reasoning.
