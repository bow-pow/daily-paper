LeWorldModel: Stable End-to-End Joint-Embedding Predictive Architecture from Pixels

Lucas Maes, Quentin Le Lidec, Damien Scieur, Yann LeCun, Randall Balestriero
The Brief

This research introduces LeWorldModel (LeWM), a new way for AI to learn about the world from raw images, like a video feed.

LeWM is a type of "world model," which is an AI's internal understanding of how things work. It achieves stable learning by predicting future visual information in a compressed "latent space" (a simplified representation) using just two simple loss functions: one for prediction accuracy and another to keep the representations nicely spread out like a Gaussian distribution. This is a significant improvement over previous methods that needed many complex loss terms or pre-trained components to prevent the AI from learning trivial or collapsed representations.

This breakthrough could lead to AI systems that learn more efficiently and understand physical concepts, potentially enabling more robust and adaptable robots or AI agents that can better anticipate and react to their environment.

Background

Imagine trying to teach a robot to navigate a room or a kitchen. The robot sees the world through cameras, just like us. To learn effectively, it needs to understand how its actions affect its surroundings. For instance, if it pushes a cup, it needs to predict where the cup will end up. This understanding of cause and effect in the environment is called a "world model." Building these world models from raw visual input (pixels) is a major goal in AI, but current methods often struggle. They can be unstable, require complex setups, or need a lot of pre-existing knowledge. The core problem is that when a model tries to predict the future from pixels, it can easily "collapse" into a state where it just outputs the same, uninformative prediction for everything, making it useless.

What they did

This research introduces LeWorldModel (LeWM), a new way to build world models that is designed to be stable and learn directly from pixels without needing complex pre-training or many tuning knobs. LeWM is a type of architecture called a Joint-Embedding Predictive Architecture (JEPA). Here's how it works:

First, an "encoder" part of the model looks at an image (a frame from a video) and turns it into a compact, low-dimensional summary called a "latent representation." Think of this like summarizing a whole paragraph into a few key bullet points.

Second, a "predictor" part takes this summary (the latent representation) from the current moment, along with an action the robot took (like "move forward"), and tries to predict the summary of what the *next* image will look like.

The key innovation is how LeWM trains these two parts together. It uses only two main "loss terms" (ways to measure how wrong the predictions are) to guide the learning: 1.

Next-embedding prediction loss

This directly penalizes the predictor if its predicted summary of the next frame is different from the actual summary of the next frame. This encourages the encoder to create summaries that the predictor can actually use to forecast the future. 2.

Gaussian-distributed latent embeddings regularizer (SIGReg)

This is the crucial part for stability. It forces the summaries (latent representations) produced by the encoder to have a specific statistical property: they should be distributed like a "standard Gaussian" (a bell curve shape in multiple dimensions). This prevents the "collapse" problem because if all summaries were the same, they wouldn't form a Gaussian distribution. It encourages diversity and structure in the learned representations.

Unlike previous methods that often needed six or more loss terms and complex techniques (like "exponential moving averages" or "stop-gradients"), LeWM uses just these two terms and a single main hyperparameter (a tuning value for the regularization strength). This makes it much simpler to train and more stable, even learning end-to-end from raw pixels.

What they found

LeWM demonstrates impressive results across various tasks. It can learn a world model with only about 15 million parameters, trainable on a single GPU in a few hours.

*

Efficiency

LeWM is significantly faster at "planning" (figuring out a sequence of actions to achieve a goal) compared to other advanced world models. It can plan up to 48 times faster, with full planning taking less than a second. *

Performance

It achieves competitive performance on diverse 2D and 3D control tasks (like pushing objects or navigating). For example, on the "Push-T" task, it achieved an 87% success rate, outperforming some baselines. *

Physical Understanding

The learned latent space captures meaningful physical properties. When researchers trained simple models to extract physical quantities (like object location) from LeWM's latent representations, they found it performed very well, often better than other end-to-end methods. *

Detecting the Unphysical

LeWM can reliably detect physically implausible events. When shown scenarios where objects teleported unexpectedly, the model registered a high "surprise" value, indicating it recognized the event as unusual or impossible according to its learned physics.

Why it matters

LeWM represents a significant step towards building more robust and efficient AI agents that can learn from raw sensory input. Its simplicity and stability, achieved through the novel use of Gaussian regularization, reduce the complexity of training world models. This makes advanced AI capabilities more accessible, as it requires less computational power and fewer expert tuning skills.

The ability to learn a world model end-to-end from pixels without relying on pre-trained models or complex heuristics is crucial for creating AI that can adapt to new environments and tasks more easily. The demonstration of physical understanding and the ability to detect unexpected events suggest that LeWM's latent representations are not just good for prediction, but also capture a deeper, more intuitive understanding of how the world works. This has implications for developing more intelligent robots, autonomous systems, and AI that can reason about the physical world. A limitation noted is that in very simple environments with low data diversity, the Gaussian prior might be less effective, suggesting future work could involve pre-training on broader datasets.
