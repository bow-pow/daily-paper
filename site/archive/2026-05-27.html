Yau's conjecture for nonlocal minimal surfaces

Michele Caselli, Enric Florit-Simon, Joaquim Serra
The Brief

This research introduces a new type of geometric object, "nonlocal minimal surfaces," and proves their existence in a wide variety of spaces.

The core idea is to generalize the concept of minimal surfaces, which are surfaces that locally minimize their area, by allowing points on the surface to "see" and influence each other from a distance, hence "nonlocal." The paper shows that in any closed, curved space (a Riemannian manifold), there are infinitely many such nonlocal minimal surfaces. Crucially, these surfaces are found to be very well-behaved, meaning they are smooth (without sharp corners or breaks) in most dimensions, and possess strong structural properties.

This work is interesting because it expands our understanding of geometric shapes in curved spaces and provides powerful new tools for studying them, potentially leading to breakthroughs in areas like understanding the topology of manifolds or even in physics where minimal surfaces appear in theories of gravity.

Here's an explainer of the research paper "Yau's conjecture for nonlocal minimal surfaces":

Background

Mathematicians have long been fascinated by the existence and properties of "minimal surfaces" within three-dimensional spaces, particularly those that are "closed" (meaning they have no boundaries and are finite in extent, like the surface of a sphere). A famous conjecture by mathematician Shing-Tung Yau proposed that every such closed three-dimensional space should contain infinitely many smooth, minimal surfaces. This problem is deeply connected to understanding the geometry of these spaces and how they can be "shaped" in the simplest possible way. The challenge lies in the fact that traditional methods for finding these surfaces can lead to complicated, non-smooth objects or objects that are hard to distinguish from each other.

What they did

This paper introduces a new concept: "nonlocal minimal surfaces." Imagine a traditional minimal surface as a soap film minimizing its surface area. A nonlocal minimal surface is like a soap film that also considers the "pull" or influence from points further away, not just its immediate neighbors. This "nonlocal" behavior is mathematically captured using a tool called the "fractional Laplacian," which is a generalization of the standard Laplacian operator used in many areas of physics and mathematics. The fractional Laplacian allows for interactions between distant points.

The researchers then developed a way to find these nonlocal minimal surfaces on any closed Riemannian manifold (a smooth, curved space with a notion of distance). They used a powerful mathematical technique called "min-max methods," which are like sophisticated optimization strategies that can find critical points of energy functionals. In this case, they applied these methods to a related energy called the "fractional Allen-Cahn energy." This energy is designed to find surfaces that are "stationary" (like a balanced soap film) and have certain desirable properties. A key aspect of their approach is to study these nonlocal surfaces as a parameter, denoted by 's', approaches 1. The case where s=1 formally corresponds to the classical minimal surfaces.

What they found

The core finding is that, just as Yau conjectured for classical minimal surfaces, every closed n-dimensional Riemannian manifold (for n ≥ 3) contains infinitely many nonlocal s-minimal surfaces for s close to 1. Specifically, they proved that for any given number of "min-max parameters" (p), they can construct infinitely many such surfaces with a "Morse index" (a measure of instability or the number of directions in which the surface's energy can be decreased) at most p. The fractional perimeter of these surfaces grows in a controlled way with p.

Crucially, they established strong regularity properties for these nonlocal minimal surfaces. For dimensions n=3 and n=4, these surfaces are smooth when s is close to 1. For higher dimensions (n ≥ 5), they are smooth everywhere except possibly on a lower-dimensional "singular set." They also proved remarkable "rigidity" properties, meaning that finite Morse index nonlocal minimal surfaces in Euclidean space (Rn) are forced to be very simple shapes, like hyperplanes (flat surfaces) or half-spaces, under certain conditions. This is a significant departure from the behavior of classical minimal surfaces.

Why it matters

This work offers a powerful new framework for studying minimal surfaces. By introducing nonlocal minimal surfaces, the researchers have created objects that are more amenable to rigorous mathematical analysis, particularly using min-max techniques. These nonlocal surfaces prevent many of the pathological behaviors (like loss of topology or multiplicity) that plague the study of classical minimal surfaces.

The fact that these nonlocal surfaces approximate classical minimal surfaces as s approaches 1 is a major breakthrough. It suggests that this new approach can be used to provide new proofs for long-standing problems, like Yau's conjecture, and to discover new minimal surfaces. The strong regularity and rigidity results for finite Morse index nonlocal minimal surfaces are particularly surprising and open up new avenues for understanding geometric objects in higher dimensions. In essence, this paper provides a more robust and versatile tool for exploring the fundamental geometry of spaces.
