Detecting low-mass haloes with strong gravitational lensing II: constraints on the density profiles of two detected subhaloes

Giulia Despali, Felix M. Heinze, Christopher D. Fassnacht, Simona Vegetti, Cristiana Spingola, Ralf Klessen
The Brief

This research investigates the nature of small, invisible clumps of matter called "subhaloes" by observing how they bend light from distant galaxies.

Using a phenomenon called strong gravitational lensing, where massive objects act like cosmic magnifying glasses, scientists analyzed two specific subhaloes. They found these subhaloes have surprisingly dense centers, meaning the matter is packed in more tightly than predicted by the standard model of dark matter, known as the cold dark matter (CDM) model. While some simulations can produce similar dense subhaloes, one of the analyzed subhaloes requires a lot of visible matter (stars) in its center, which contradicts what was observed.

This work helps refine our understanding of dark matter, the mysterious substance that makes up most of the universe's mass, and how it behaves on smaller scales.

Background

The universe is filled with dark matter, an invisible substance that interacts only through gravity. While we can't see it directly, its gravitational pull affects visible matter. One way to study dark matter is by observing how its gravity bends light from distant galaxies, a phenomenon called gravitational lensing. This bending can reveal the presence of not just large clumps of dark matter, but also smaller "subhalos" orbiting within larger galaxies. Scientists are particularly interested in these low-mass subhalos because their abundance and properties can tell us a lot about the fundamental nature of dark matter. The standard model of dark matter, known as Cold Dark Matter (CDM), predicts a certain distribution and structure for these subhalos. However, some observations of these subhalos have shown properties that seem to deviate from these predictions, creating a puzzle for physicists. This research aims to investigate two specific instances where such low-mass subhalos were detected using gravitational lensing, to see if their observed properties are truly at odds with the CDM model.

What they did

The researchers focused on two specific gravitational lensing systems, J0946+1006 and JVASB1938+666, where previous studies had reported the detection of low-mass subhalos. They used sophisticated computer modeling techniques to analyze the light from the lensed background galaxies. By carefully fitting these models to the observed distorted images, they could infer the mass distribution of the objects causing the lensing. To understand the shape and density of the subhalos, they tested four different mathematical models for their "density profiles" – essentially, how the density of matter changes with distance from the center. These models included the standard Navarro-Frenk-White (NFW) profile, a Pseudo-Jaffe (PJ) profile, and a general Power-Law (PL) profile. They then compared the properties of the subhalos inferred from these models to the predictions from a state-of-the-art cosmological simulation called IllustrisTNG50-1. This simulation includes both dark matter and the complex effects of normal matter (like stars and gas) on the structure of these subhalos.

What they found

The analysis revealed that the two detected subhalos are best described by density profiles with inner slopes that are "steep," meaning the density increases rapidly towards the center, similar to or even steeper than an "isothermal" profile (where density decreases linearly with radius). The standard NFW profile, a common prediction from dark matter-only simulations, requires extremely high "concentrations" (a measure of how compact a halo is) to match these observations, making them outliers compared to typical CDM predictions. Interestingly, the researchers found a characteristic radius within which different best-fit density profiles predict the same enclosed mass. This radius appears to be more robustly constrained by the lensing data than the precise inner slope. When comparing to the IllustrisTNG50-1 simulation, which includes baryonic physics, the diversity of simulated subhalo profiles, shaped by tidal stripping and baryonic effects, can indeed produce these steep inner slopes. This helps to alleviate some of the tension with previous findings. However, while simulated subhalos could match the detection in JVASB1938+666, the amount of stellar material needed in simulations to explain the central density of the subhalo in J0946+1006 appears to be in conflict with the observational limits on its luminosity.

Why it matters

This research provides a more detailed look at the properties of low-mass dark matter subhalos detected through gravitational lensing. It highlights that while the standard NFW profile might not be the best description for these observed objects, more complex models that account for baryonic physics and tidal effects, as seen in advanced simulations like TNG50-1, can better reproduce the observed steep density profiles. This is crucial because it suggests that the apparent discrepancies with the CDM model might be resolved by considering the full complexity of subhalo formation and evolution, rather than necessarily pointing to a fundamental flaw in the CDM paradigm itself. However, the specific case of J0946+1006 still presents a challenge, indicating that either our understanding of the subhalo's stellar content or the observational constraints needs further refinement. The study emphasizes the importance of increasing the number of such detections to build a larger statistical sample, which will be essential for revealing more about the detailed structure of these elusive objects and ultimately shedding light on the nature of dark matter in the universe.
