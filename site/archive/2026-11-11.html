On anomalous diffusion in the Kraichnan model and correlated-in-time variants

Keefer Rowan
The Brief

This research investigates how a passive substance, like a dye in turbulent water, spreads out in a specific, idealized model of turbulence.

The core finding is that in a simplified model where the turbulent flow is random and instantaneous in time (called "white-in-time"), a dye will spread out unusually quickly, faster than normal diffusion, a phenomenon termed "anomalous diffusion." This happens even if the dye has some resistance to spreading (diffusivity) and regardless of the initial dye pattern. The paper also shows that if the turbulence has memory (correlated-in-time), this anomalous spreading might disappear, and surprisingly, anomalous diffusion is linked to the fact that the paths of fluid particles in such flows can be unpredictable and not uniquely defined.

This work is interesting because it sheds light on the fundamental ways things spread in chaotic systems, which has implications for understanding phenomena ranging from pollutant dispersal in the atmosphere to the mixing of chemicals.

Background

Imagine trying to understand how heat spreads through a turbulent fluid. Turbulence is incredibly complex, with swirling eddies and unpredictable motion. A key phenomenon observed in turbulent fluids is "anomalous dissipation," where energy seems to disappear faster than expected, even when viscosity (which causes dissipation) is very low. This is a fundamental puzzle in physics. To simplify, researchers study a related problem: how a passive substance, like a dye or temperature, spreads within a turbulent flow. This is called "scalar turbulence." In this simpler setting, scientists look for "anomalous diffusion," which is like anomalous dissipation but for the spreading of the passive substance. Specifically, they want to know if the substance still spreads out quickly even when the fluid's "viscosity" (which normally smooths things out) is almost zero. Finding rigorous mathematical examples of this anomalous diffusion has been very challenging, especially for flows that are "rough" or chaotic.

What they did

This research uses a mathematical model called the Kraichnan model to study anomalous diffusion. In this model, the turbulent flow is represented by a special type of random mathematical object called a "stochastic partial differential equation" (SPDE). This flow is "white-in-time," meaning its behavior at any given moment is independent of its past. This is a simplification, as real-world flows have some memory. The researchers developed a new, concise mathematical proof using techniques from the theory of partial differential equations (PDEs) to show that this Kraichnan model exhibits anomalous diffusion. They specifically analyzed how a passive scalar (like temperature) spreads when advected by this random flow. Furthermore, they explored what happens when the "white-in-time" assumption is relaxed, and the flow has some memory (is "correlated-in-time"). They constructed examples of such correlated-in-time flows and showed that, surprisingly, they *don't* exhibit anomalous diffusion, even though their idealized "white-in-time" limits *do*. A key part of their argument involves proving that if anomalous diffusion occurs, the underlying flow must have non-unique trajectories (meaning different paths can be followed by particles in the flow, even if they start very close together).

What they found

The main result is a rigorous proof that the Kraichnan model, under certain conditions on its parameters (specifically, when the flow is "rough" enough, meaning its spatial regularity parameter $\alpha$ is less than 1), exhibits anomalous diffusion. This means the expected squared $L^2$ norm (a measure of the overall "energy" or spread of the passive scalar) decays exponentially over time, with a rate that is independent of the diffusivity $\kappa$ (which represents viscosity) and the small-scale cutoff $\eta$ of the flow. The decay rate is given by $e^{-t/C}$ for some constant $C$, and the prefactor depends on $\alpha$, $\kappa$, and $\eta$ in a specific way: $\exp\left( - C \frac{1-\alpha}{\log \kappa / \log \eta + 1} \right)$. Crucially, anomalous diffusion is observed in the limit where both the small-scale cutoff $\eta$ and diffusivity $\kappa$ go to zero. The paper also demonstrates that introducing time correlations into the flow can destroy anomalous diffusion. They provide examples of correlated-in-time models that, for any fixed time correlation, do not show anomalous diffusion, even though their formal white-in-time limits do. Finally, they prove that anomalous diffusion implies non-uniqueness of the trajectories of the advecting flow.

Why it matters

This work provides a rigorous mathematical foundation for understanding anomalous diffusion in a simplified model of turbulence. The PDE-based proof is accessible to a broader scientific audience, including those in fluid dynamics. The finding that the "white-in-time" property is essential for anomalous diffusion in this model is significant. It suggests that the instantaneous, uncorrelated nature of the random forcing is key to generating the rapid spreading, and that realistic flows with some temporal memory might behave differently. The construction of correlated-in-time models that *lack* anomalous diffusion highlights the delicate balance of properties required for this phenomenon. The proof that anomalous diffusion implies non-unique flow trajectories is a powerful theoretical link, connecting the spreading of passive scalars to the fundamental nature of the fluid's motion. For a non-specialist, this research contributes to the ongoing effort to mathematically explain complex phenomena observed in turbulent systems, moving beyond heuristic arguments to rigorous proofs. It underscores that even simplified models can reveal deep insights into the behavior of chaotic systems.
