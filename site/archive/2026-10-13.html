The progenitor of SN 2023ixf from hydrodynamical modelling

M. C. Bersten, M. Orellana, G. Folatelli, L. Martinez, M. P. Piccirilli, T. Regna et al.
The Brief

This research investigates the identity of the star that exploded as supernova SN 2023ixf, one of the closest stellar explosions seen in decades. By simulating how stars of different masses explode and comparing these simulations to the observed light and expansion of SN 2023ixf, the study pinpoints the progenitor star. The findings suggest the star was relatively small, with an initial mass of around 12 times that of our Sun, and its explosion was typical, releasing energy and producing a standard amount of radioactive nickel. Understanding the nature of these progenitor stars helps us piece together the life cycles of stars and how they end, ultimately shaping the universe we observe.

Background

Supernovae (SNe) are cataclysmic explosions of stars, and understanding their origins is crucial for astrophysics. SN 2023ixf is a particularly interesting event because it's one of the closest "Type II" supernovae observed in decades. This proximity means astronomers have a wealth of data, both from the explosion itself and from images taken *before* the star exploded. The challenge is to definitively identify the type of star that exploded (its "progenitor") and its initial mass. Previous studies using pre-explosion images have yielded a wide range of estimates for the progenitor's mass, from about 8 to over 20 times the mass of our Sun. This paper aims to provide a more independent and precise determination of the progenitor's properties by analyzing the supernova's light curve and expansion velocities.

What they did

The core of this research involves creating computer simulations of stellar explosions. To do this, the researchers first used a sophisticated stellar evolution code (called MESA) to model how stars of different initial masses would evolve over their lifetimes, up to the point just before they explode. They specifically modeled stars that would eventually become Type II supernovae, focusing on those with initial masses (Zero Age Main Sequence mass, or $M_\mathrm{ZAMS}$) of 12, 15, 20, and 22 times the Sun's mass.

Then, they took these pre-explosion stellar models and simulated their explosions. These simulations are based on "hydrodynamics," which is the study of how fluids (in this case, the star's material) move and interact under pressure and forces. They calculated the resulting "bolometric light curve" – essentially, the total energy output of the supernova across all wavelengths of light over time – and the "expansion velocities" of the supernova's ejected material.

The key step was to compare these simulated light curves and velocities with the actual observational data from SN 2023ixf. By adjusting parameters like the explosion energy and the amount of radioactive material produced (specifically, Nickel-56, denoted as $^{56}$Ni), they sought to find a model that best matched the observed supernova behavior, particularly during the "plateau phase" (when the light curve stays relatively constant) and the subsequent "radioactive tail" (when the light curve declines as radioactive elements decay).

What they found

The simulations revealed that the explosion of a star with an initial mass ($M_\mathrm{ZAMS}$) of 12 times the Sun's mass provided the best match to the observed data for SN 2023ixf, specifically for times after about 20 days from the explosion. This best-fit model required an explosion energy of $1.2 \times 10^{51}$ erg and a production of 0.05 solar masses of Nickel-56. The pre-explosion star in this model had a mass of 10.9 solar masses and a radius of 720 times the Sun's radius. This indicates that SN 2023ixf was a "normal" Type II supernova event. Importantly, their modeling suggested an upper limit for the progenitor's initial mass of $M_\mathrm{ZAMS} < 15$ solar masses, strongly favoring the lower end of the mass range previously estimated from pre-explosion observations.

Why it matters

This research provides a crucial, independent way to determine the properties of a supernova progenitor, complementing direct observations of the star before it exploded. The wide range of previous mass estimates for SN 2023ixf's progenitor highlighted the need for such independent methods. By successfully modeling the supernova's light curve and expansion velocities, this study narrows down the likely initial mass of the progenitor star to below 15 solar masses, and specifically points to a 12 solar mass star as the most probable origin. This helps resolve discrepancies in earlier studies and contributes to our understanding of which types of stars end their lives as Type II supernovae. While the models match the later stages of the supernova well, the very early emission (before 10-20 days) is not perfectly reproduced, suggesting the presence of circumstellar material (material ejected by the star before exploding) that interacts with the supernova ejecta. A full understanding of this interaction is left for future work. Nevertheless, the main conclusion about the progenitor's mass remains robust.
