What Improves During Pretraining? Tracking Knowledge Acquisition with Point-in-Time Models

Chang Su, Bin Wu, Emine Yilmaz, Yao Lu
The Brief

This research investigates how large language models learn different types of knowledge as they are trained on vast amounts of text data collected over several years.

The core idea is to track how the "prevalence" or commonness of specific pieces of information changes across different years of web data. They developed a "temporal variability score" (TVS) to measure this; a high TVS means a piece of knowledge appears inconsistently across years. They found that knowledge that is less consistent over time (high TVS) is actually learned *faster* later in the training process and ultimately leads to better performance on knowledge-heavy tasks.

This is interesting because it shows that language models don't just learn everything at once; they prioritize and learn different kinds of information at different stages, which could help us build better, more efficient AI.

Here's an explainer of the research paper "What Improves During Pretraining? Tracking Knowledge Acquisition with Point-in-Time Models":

Background

Large language models, like the ones powering many AI applications today, learn by reading vast amounts of text from the internet. This pretraining process helps them acquire knowledge and become proficient at various tasks. However, it's not entirely clear *how* they learn different types of knowledge as they progress through this massive training. Do they learn all facts equally well from the start, or do some facts take longer to grasp? This research aims to understand the learning trajectory of different kinds of knowledge during pretraining.

What they did

The researchers tackled this question by using a clever technique involving "point-in-time" (PIT) language models. Imagine having several versions of a language model, each trained on a snapshot of the internet from a different year (e.g., 2017, 2018, ..., 2024). These PIT models share the same underlying structure and training methods, but their training data differs only in the year it was collected. This allows researchers to see how a model's "understanding" of a piece of information changes depending on when that information was prevalent on the web.

They developed a metric called the "temporal variability score" (TVS). For a specific fact or question-answer pair, TVS measures how much the PIT models disagree on how likely that item is. If a fact is consistently present and phrased similarly across many years, models trained on different yearly snapshots will likely assign it a similar likelihood. If a fact's prevalence or phrasing changes dramatically year to year, the models will show more variation in their likelihood assignments. A high TVS means the knowledge is "temporally variable" (its presence or form changes over time), while a low TVS means it's "temporally stable."

They then applied this TVS metric to individual questions from various knowledge-based benchmarks (like those testing factual recall, reasoning, and common sense). They grouped these questions into low-TVS and high-TVS categories. Finally, they tracked how well different language models (from various open-source families) performed on these low- and high-TVS questions at different stages of their own pretraining.

What they found

The study revealed a distinct pattern in how knowledge is learned. Early in the pretraining process, models performed similarly on both temporally stable (low-TVS) and temporally variable (high-TVS) knowledge items. However, as pretraining progressed, the models improved much faster on the temporally variable (high-TVS) items. By the end of the training, high-TVS items were answered correctly more often than low-TVS items in a significant majority of cases (34 out of 36 model-benchmark settings), with an average advantage of 7.3 points in chance-normalized accuracy. This effect was particularly strong on tasks that heavily rely on knowledge.

Furthermore, they found that the *answer* itself, not just the question's wording, plays a crucial role. When they isolated the variability of the correct answer compared to incorrect ones, this "answer-specific TVS" became an even stronger predictor of later learning gains.

Why it matters

This research provides a valuable new tool, TVS, for understanding what and how language models learn during pretraining. It moves beyond simply looking at overall performance on benchmarks and offers a more granular view of knowledge acquisition. The findings suggest that while models might initially struggle with information that fluctuates in its online presence, they become particularly adept at learning and utilizing this type of knowledge later in their training. This has implications for how we design training data, evaluate models, and understand their capabilities. The limitations include the reliance on the quality of the PIT models and the fact that the study focused on multiple-choice benchmarks, which might not fully represent all types of knowledge acquisition. Nevertheless, TVS offers a promising way to diagnose learning dynamics that aggregate scores can miss.
