ResearchRubrics: A Benchmark of Prompts and Rubrics For Evaluating Deep Research Agents

Manasi Sharma, Chen Bo Calvin Zhang, Chaithanya Bandi, Clinton Wang, Ankit Aich, Huy Nghiem et al.
The Brief

This research introduces a new way to test how well AI systems can do "deep research" – answering complex questions by finding and combining information from many sources.

The core idea is creating a standardized test called ResearchRubrics, which includes over 2,800 hours of human work to develop realistic questions and detailed scoring guides (rubrics). These rubrics assess if the AI's answers are factually correct, logically sound, and clearly written, even when dealing with dynamic information. A key finding is that even advanced AI systems struggle, scoring below 68% on average, often missing subtle context or failing to properly connect retrieved information.

This matters because it shows how far AI still has to go before it can reliably act as a trustworthy research assistant for everyday tasks, from writing reports to understanding complex topics.

Background

Imagine you ask a super-smart AI assistant to research a complex topic for you, like "What are the long-term societal impacts of social media?" This isn't a simple question with a single right answer you can find in a quick search. It requires the AI to dig through many sources, understand different perspectives, connect ideas, and then explain it all in a detailed, well-supported report. These kinds of advanced AI assistants are called "Deep Research" (DR) agents.

The problem is, how do we know if these DR agents are actually doing a good job? Evaluating them is really hard. Their answers are long, can be phrased in many different ways, and might draw on information that's constantly changing online. Existing ways to test AI, which often look for short, factual answers, just don't cut it for this kind of deep, nuanced research. We need a better way to measure if these agents can truly understand, synthesize, and present complex information reliably.

What they did

To solve this evaluation problem, the researchers created "ResearchRubrics." Think of it as a comprehensive testing ground for DR agents. They spent over 2,800 hours creating a set of realistic research questions (called "prompts") that cover a wide variety of topics, from business to history to everyday consumer questions. For each prompt, they didn't just ask for an answer; they developed very detailed "rubrics." These rubrics are like grading checklists, written by human experts, that specify exactly what a good answer should include. They look at things like:

*

Factual Grounding

Is the information accurate and supported by evidence? *

Reasoning Soundness

Does the AI's thinking process make sense, connecting ideas logically? *

Clarity

Is the answer easy to understand and well-organized?

Crucially, these rubrics are not generated by other AIs, which could lead to biased or circular evaluations. They are written and reviewed by humans to ensure they capture the subtle requirements of good research.

They also developed a way to categorize the complexity of these research questions along three axes: how many different topics are involved (conceptual breadth), how many steps of reasoning are needed (logical nesting depth), and how open-ended or underspecified the question is (exploration level). This helps them understand where AIs struggle most.

Finally, they created methods for both humans and other AIs (called "LLM-as-a-judge") to score the DR agents' responses against these detailed rubrics. They even explored different scoring methods, like giving full credit, partial credit, or no credit for each rubric item (ternary grading vs. binary grading).

What they found

The results were eye-opening. Even the top-performing DR agents, like Google's Gemini DR and OpenAI's DR, only managed to comply with the rubrics about 67-68% of the time. When using a stricter "binary" grading (meaning only fully met or not met), this dropped to around 60-61%. This shows that current advanced AI research assistants still have significant room for improvement.

The biggest areas where these agents faltered were

implicit reasoning

(understanding what's implied but not explicitly stated) and

synthesis of information

(combining information from multiple sources into a coherent whole). They were much better at tasks that involved explicit instructions or clear communication.

Performance also decreased significantly as the complexity of the task increased. Agents struggled more with questions requiring many steps of reasoning (deep logical nesting) and those that were highly open-ended. Interestingly, they performed better on some "consulting" type questions than on highly technical ones, which goes against what one might intuitively expect.

Why it matters

This work is important because it provides the first standardized, expert-driven benchmark for evaluating the complex capabilities of Deep Research agents. Before ResearchRubrics, it was hard to get a clear, objective picture of how well these agents could actually perform real-world research tasks.

The findings highlight that while current DR agents are impressive, they are not yet reliable for tasks requiring deep understanding and nuanced reasoning. The fact that even leading systems miss implicit context and struggle with synthesizing information means they can't be fully trusted to provide comprehensive and accurate research without human oversight. This is crucial for anyone considering using these tools for important work.

The researchers suggest that simply tweaking prompts (prompt engineering) might not be enough to fix these issues. The systematic failures point towards fundamental limitations in the underlying architecture of these AI models. This implies that significant architectural innovation is needed to create truly capable and trustworthy AI research assistants. For a non-specialist, this means that while AI is getting better at research, it's still a tool that needs careful supervision and validation, especially for complex or critical tasks. The release of ResearchRubrics itself is a significant contribution, as it allows other researchers to build upon this work and accelerate progress in developing more reliable AI research partners.
