Self-Evolving Curriculum for LLM Reasoning

Xiaoyin Chen, Jiarui Lu, Minsu Kim, Dinghuai Zhang, Jian Tang, Alexandre Piché et al.
The Brief

This research introduces a new way to teach large language models (LLMs) how to reason better by automatically figuring out the best order to present them with practice problems.

The core idea is to treat the selection of problem types as a game where the model learns which types of problems are most helpful for its progress. They use a technique called reinforcement learning (RL), where the model gets "rewards" for learning effectively. The system learns a "curriculum policy" – essentially a strategy for picking problem categories – at the same time it's improving its reasoning skills. This curriculum policy aims to maximize the immediate learning gain, measured by something called "absolute advantage."

This is interesting because it could lead to LLMs that are much better at solving complex problems, like math or logic puzzles, without needing humans to carefully design every step of their training.

Background

Large language models (LLMs) are powerful tools for tasks like math and coding, but they can struggle with complex reasoning. Researchers have found that a technique called reinforcement learning (RL) can significantly improve these reasoning skills. However, the effectiveness of RL fine-tuning depends heavily on *how* the training problems are presented to the model. Imagine teaching a student: showing them only the hardest problems first might be overwhelming, while showing them only the easiest might not push them to learn new skills. This order of presenting problems is called a "curriculum." While random ordering is a common starting point, it's not optimal. Manually designing a good curriculum is difficult and requires a lot of expert knowledge, and automatically filtering problems during training can be too slow and computationally expensive. This research aims to solve this problem by creating an automatic way to learn the best training order.

What they did

The core idea is to create a "Self-Evolving Curriculum" (SEC) that learns the optimal training order *while* the LLM is being fine-tuned with reinforcement learning. They frame this problem as a "Multi-Armed Bandit" (MAB) problem. Think of a bandit machine with multiple levers (arms), where each lever represents a category of training problems (e.g., easy math problems, medium planning problems, hard inductive reasoning problems). The goal is to figure out which lever to pull (which category of problems to train on) to get the best reward.

In this context, the "reward" for pulling an arm (selecting a problem category) is a measure of how much the LLM is expected to learn from it. The researchers use a concept called "absolute advantage" from reinforcement learning as a proxy for this learning gain. Essentially, problems that are neither too easy nor too hard for the LLM at a given moment are likely to provide the biggest learning boost. The SEC system has a "curriculum policy" that decides which problem categories to sample from at each training step, aiming to maximize this expected learning gain. This curriculum policy is updated continuously using a method called TD(0), which is a standard technique for learning from sequential data. So, the LLM learns to reason better, and at the same time, the system learns how to present the problems to the LLM in the most effective order.

What they found

Experiments across three different reasoning domains—planning (like logic puzzles), inductive reasoning (finding patterns), and mathematics—showed that SEC significantly improves the LLM's reasoning abilities. Crucially, SEC enabled models to generalize better to harder problems that were not seen during training ("out-of-distribution" problems). For instance, on the Countdown game, SEC improved out-of-distribution accuracy by about 13% compared to a random curriculum. For the challenging AIME math dataset, SEC achieved a relative improvement of about 33% over the random baseline.

SEC also proved effective when the LLM was trained simultaneously on multiple reasoning domains. It helped balance the learning across different tasks, preventing one task from being neglected while others improved. The research also showed that SEC adapts the difficulty of problems presented over time, starting with easier problems and gradually introducing harder ones as the LLM's capabilities grow, which is a key aspect of effective learning.

Why it matters

This work introduces a practical and automatic way to improve how LLMs learn complex reasoning skills. Instead of relying on human experts to design tricky training schedules or using computationally expensive methods, SEC offers an adaptive solution. This means LLMs can become better at tasks requiring logical deduction, pattern recognition, and problem-solving more efficiently. The ability to generalize to unseen, harder problems is particularly important for real-world applications where LLMs might encounter novel situations. The finding that SEC can balance learning across multiple skills simultaneously is also a significant step towards building more versatile AI systems. While SEC introduces some new parameters to tune, its ability to automatically discover effective learning pathways makes it a promising direction for future LLM development, potentially leading to more capable and robust AI.
