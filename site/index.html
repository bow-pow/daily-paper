Entanglement and HBAR entropy in a Lorentzian non-commutative black hole

A. A. Araújo Filho, N. Heidari, P. H. M. Barros, V. B. Bezerra, A. R. Queiroz, A. F. Santos et al.
The Brief

This research explores how a specific modification to the structure of spacetime near a black hole influences quantum phenomena like entanglement and a particular type of radiation.

The key finding is that introducing "non-commutative smearing" – a way of fuzzing out the precise location of spacetime points – alters how quantum information is shared between the inside and outside of a black hole. This smearing reduces the black hole's "surface gravity" (a measure of how strongly gravity pulls at its edge), increases the correlations between quantum particles, and affects the probability of emitting radiation. The way this smearing impacts the black hole's properties, like its mass and the radiation it emits, is also detailed.

This work is interesting because it connects abstract ideas about quantum mechanics and gravity in extreme environments, potentially offering insights into the fundamental nature of black holes and the information paradox.

Background

Imagine a black hole, a region of spacetime so dense that nothing, not even light, can escape. For decades, physicists have understood that black holes aren't entirely black; they emit a faint thermal radiation, known as Hawking radiation, due to quantum effects near their event horizon (the point of no return). This radiation carries information about the black hole and is crucial for understanding the "information paradox" – the puzzle of what happens to information that falls into a black hole. This research delves into how certain modifications to the fabric of spacetime, specifically a concept called "non-commutative geometry," might alter this quantum behavior, affecting both the entanglement of quantum fields and the radiation emitted by black holes.

What they did

The researchers explored how a specific type of "smearing" or smoothing of the black hole's mass distribution, called "Lorentzian non-commutative smearing," influences quantum phenomena near the black hole. They treated the black hole as a region of spacetime described by a modified metric (a mathematical tool that defines distances and geometry). This smearing introduces a new length scale, denoted by $\Theta$, which effectively "smooths out" the black hole's singularity.

They analyzed two main aspects: 1.

Entanglement

They investigated how quantum entanglement, a bizarre connection between quantum particles where they remain linked no matter the distance, is affected. Specifically, they looked at the entanglement between quantum fields on the exterior of the black hole and their "partner" fields that would be inside the black hole. By "tracing over" (effectively ignoring) the interior partners, they could quantify the entanglement accessible to an outside observer. They used measures like logarithmic negativity and mutual information to do this. 2.

Horizon Brightened Acceleration Radiation (HBAR)

They examined the radiation emitted by a hypothetical atom falling into the black hole. This radiation, influenced by the atom's acceleration and the black hole's gravity, is related to Hawking radiation. They calculated the probabilities of the atom emitting or absorbing quanta of the scalar field and how these probabilities change with the smearing. They also explored the entropy production associated with this radiation, linking it to changes in the black hole's area.

What they found

The introduction of Lorentzian non-commutative smearing has several key effects: *

Entanglement

Increasing the smearing length ($\ell$) generally *increases* the accessible entanglement (measured by logarithmic negativity and mutual information) for quantum fields outside the black hole. For instance, at a fixed mass and frequency, increasing smearing leads to more correlations between the exterior field modes. *

Surface Gravity and Temperature

Smearing causes the black hole's surface gravity ($\kappa$) and thus its Hawking temperature ($T_H$) to decrease. This effect begins at order $\sqrt{\Theta}$. *

Atomic Emission

For a freely falling atom, the smearing suppresses the probability of emitting radiation at a given frequency. However, the emission and absorption processes still obey detailed balance at the modified Hawking temperature. *

Entropy and Area

The relationship between the entropy of the emitted radiation (HBAR) and the change in the black hole's area is modified. When the radiated energy is treated as "horizon heat," the entropy rate is approximately one-quarter of the radiative area loss ($\dot{S}_{HBAR} \simeq \dot{A}_p/4$). However, if the radiated energy is attributed to the black hole's mass loss, the relationship becomes $\dot{S}_{HBAR} \simeq \dot{A}_p/[4(1-\ell/r_+)]$, where $\ell$ is related to the smearing and $r_+$ is the horizon radius. This indicates that the mass dependence of the exterior source enters the entropy per unit area lost at order $\sqrt{\Theta}$.

Why it matters

This research provides a deeper understanding of how quantum mechanics and gravity interact in extreme environments like black holes, especially when considering modifications to spacetime geometry. The findings suggest that smoothing out the black hole's singularity might enhance quantum correlations outside the event horizon. Furthermore, it clarifies how such modifications affect the fundamental process of black hole radiation and its thermodynamic properties, like entropy. The work connects theoretical concepts like non-commutative geometry to observable (though currently unobservable) phenomena like Hawking radiation and entanglement, offering insights into the nature of spacetime at its most fundamental level and potentially shedding light on the black hole information paradox. The specific quantitative results, like the order of corrections ($\sqrt{\Theta}$ and $\Theta$), provide concrete predictions for future theoretical or experimental investigations.
