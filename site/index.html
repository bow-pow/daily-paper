Mercury (Hg): a relativistic $N$-body simulation for Horndeski gravity

Ahmad Nouri-Zonoz, Farbod Hassani, Julian Adamek, Emilio Bellini, Martin Kunz
The Brief

This research introduces "Mercury," a new computer simulation designed to study how gravity works in the universe under a specific, more complex theory called Horndeski gravity.

Instead of just simulating how matter clumps together under standard gravity, Mercury also tracks an extra "scalar field" that is part of Horndeski gravity. This field influences gravity itself, and its behavior is described by four functions ($α_{\rm K}, α_{\rm B}, α_{\rm M}, α_{\rm T}$) and a changing "effective Planck mass" ($M_*^2$). The simulation shows how the clumping of matter (nonlinear structure formation) affects this scalar field, which in turn alters gravity and how things like galaxies form. It can handle different versions of Horndeski gravity, including those where the scalar field interacts strongly with itself.

This work is interesting because it allows scientists to explore alternative theories of gravity beyond Einstein's well-tested but potentially incomplete model, which could lead to a deeper understanding of dark energy and the evolution of the universe.

Background

Cosmologists want to understand how the universe evolved from a nearly uniform state after the Big Bang to the complex web of galaxies and clusters we see today. This evolution is driven by gravity, but also influenced by mysterious components like dark energy, which is causing the universe's expansion to accelerate. Standard models of cosmology, like the Lambda-CDM model, describe this evolution using Einstein's theory of General Relativity and assume the universe is dominated by dark matter and dark energy. However, some theories propose that gravity itself might be modified, or that dark energy is not a simple constant but a dynamic field. To test these alternative theories, especially those involving a dynamic "scalar field" that interacts with gravity, scientists need sophisticated computer simulations that can track the universe's evolution on both large and small scales, accounting for all the complex interactions. This research tackles the challenge of building such a simulation tool for a specific class of modified gravity theories called Horndeski gravity.

What they did

The researchers developed a new computer code named "Mercury" designed to simulate the universe's evolution within the framework of Horndeski gravity. This theory is a broad category of scalar-tensor theories, meaning it involves not only the gravitational field (described by the metric) but also an additional scalar field. Horndeski gravity is particularly interesting because it's the most general theory of this type that avoids certain theoretical instabilities while still allowing for modifications to gravity and dark energy.

Mercury builds upon existing simulation techniques, specifically a "particle-mesh" scheme, which represents matter as discrete particles and the gravitational field on a grid. The key innovation in Mercury is its ability to incorporate the dynamics of the extra scalar field and how it modifies Einstein's equations of gravity. They achieved this by expressing these modifications using four time-dependent functions (αK, αB, αM, αT) and an "effective Planck mass" (M*²). These parameters essentially quantify how the scalar field affects gravity and the expansion of the universe.

The code evolves the scalar field and its associated "stress-energy tensor" (which describes its energy, momentum, and pressure) alongside the matter and metric fields. This means that as structures like galaxies form (nonlinear structure formation), they influence the scalar field, which in turn feeds back and alters the gravitational potentials. Mercury can handle these theories in two ways: either by directly inputting phenomenological descriptions of the α-functions, or by starting with a specific mathematical "Lagrangian" (a fundamental description of the theory) and deriving the α-functions from it. They demonstrated this by simulating models like nKGB, Cubic Galileon, and Brans-Dicke gravity.

What they found

The researchers validated Mercury by comparing its results with a well-established linear code called hi_class. In the linear regime (where the universe's evolution is relatively simple and predictable), Mercury accurately reproduced the predictions of hi_class. They also confirmed that Mercury correctly captures known limits of specific Horndeski models, such as k-essence and kinetic gravity braiding (KGB), in the nonlinear regime.

A key finding is how the "running effective Planck mass" (meaning M*² changes over time) in these "luminal" Horndeski models generates "gravitational slip." This slip is a difference between how gravitational potentials evolve in different directions, caused by the scalar field's "anisotropic stress" (unequal pressure in different directions). This effect, along with modifications to how vector and tensor metric perturbations (like gravitational waves) are damped, is a distinct signature of these theories. Furthermore, the evolution of M*² alters the overall strength and scale-dependence of the "effective dark-energy density contrast," leaving an imprint on its power spectrum, which is a statistical measure of how matter is distributed.

Why it matters

Mercury represents a significant step towards simulating complex modified gravity theories on nonlinear scales. Current and future astronomical surveys are gathering vast amounts of data on the large-scale structure of the universe, which can be used to test cosmological models. To fully exploit this data, especially for theories beyond the standard Lambda-CDM model, simulations that can accurately capture the nonlinear evolution of gravity and dark energy are crucial.

This code provides a framework to explore how Horndeski gravity, with its dynamic scalar field, affects the formation of cosmic structures and the propagation of gravitational waves. The specific effects they identified, like gravitational slip and modified damping of perturbations, are potential observational signatures that future telescopes could look for. While Mercury is an intermediate step (it doesn't yet include all possible nonlinear self-interactions of the scalar field or "screening effects" that can make modified gravity behave like General Relativity in dense regions), it lays the groundwork for more comprehensive nonlinear simulations of these fascinating alternative gravity theories. This allows scientists to better constrain or even discover new physics governing the universe.
