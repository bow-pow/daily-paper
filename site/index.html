Solid-that-flows picture of glass-forming liquids

Jeppe C. Dyre
The Brief

This paper proposes a new way to understand how liquids that can form glasses behave as they cool down.

The core idea is that as these liquids get very cold and thick, approaching the "glass transition" where they solidify, they start acting more like solids than typical liquids. This "solidity" isn't about being rigid at the atomic level, but rather about how large groups of molecules move together over distances of about a micron (a millionth of a meter). This is a significant departure from standard liquid theories that describe faster, more fluid materials.

This perspective is interesting because it offers a simpler, more intuitive picture for a common phenomenon: why everyday materials like window glass go from liquid to solid without crystallizing, and it could help explain their unique properties.

Background

The everyday understanding of liquids is that they flow freely, while solids resist deformation. However, when certain liquids are cooled down significantly, they become incredibly viscous, flowing only at an imperceptible rate. This state, just before they solidify into a glass, presents a puzzle: are these ultra-viscous liquids just liquids with extremely high viscosity, or are they fundamentally behaving more like solids? This research explores this question, particularly focusing on the transition to the glassy state, where viscosity can increase by a staggering factor of $10^{12}$ to $10^{15}$ compared to ordinary liquids.

What they did

This paper argues that glass-forming liquids, as they approach the glass transition, behave less like conventional liquids and more like solids that can still flow, albeit very slowly. The core idea is to consider the dynamics of these liquids not as continuous flow, but as a series of discrete "flow events." These events are like tiny, localized rearrangements of particles. The paper proposes a concept called the "solidity length" ($l_s$), which is a characteristic length scale (around one micron at the glass transition) below which the liquid behaves like a solid. On length scales smaller than $l_s$, the liquid's structure and dynamics are governed by solid-like elastic properties. The paper reviews evidence from simulations and experiments that support this "solid-that-flows" picture. It discusses how these flow events, similar to defects in a solid, induce stress and strain in their surroundings, and how these interactions influence the overall behavior of the liquid. The paper also examines how this picture explains observed phenomena like "dynamic heterogeneity" (regions of fast and slow movement within the liquid) and the characteristic shapes of relaxation curves.

What they found

The central finding is that ultra-viscous liquids, below a certain length scale called the "solidity length" ($l_s$), exhibit solid-like behavior. This length scale is estimated to be around one micron at the glass transition. Below this length, the dynamics are dominated by discrete "flow events," which are localized rearrangements of particles. These events are analogous to the movement of point defects in a solid. The paper provides a formula for the solidity length: $l_s^4 \sim c a^3 \tau_\alpha$, where $c$ is the sound velocity, $a$ is the average interparticle distance, and $\tau_\alpha$ is the average relaxation time. For ordinary liquids, this length is on the order of atomic size ($10^{-10}$ m), but for glass-forming liquids near the glass transition, it grows to about $10^{-6}$ m. The research also suggests that these flow events are not independent; one event can influence the likelihood of nearby events, a phenomenon called "elastic facilitation." This facilitation helps explain the broad distribution of relaxation times observed in experiments. Furthermore, the paper proposes a "double-percolation scenario" to explain the frequency-dependent response functions of these liquids, where "islands of mobility" and "islands of immobility" play crucial roles.

Why it matters

This research offers a new conceptual framework for understanding the complex behavior of glass-forming liquids, moving beyond the traditional view of them as simply very viscous liquids. The "solid-that-flows" picture suggests that at the microscopic level, these materials possess solid-like characteristics, with dynamics driven by discrete rearrangement events rather than continuous molecular motion. This perspective has significant implications for predicting and controlling the properties of glasses, which are ubiquitous in modern technology (e.g., window panes, optical fibers, pharmaceuticals). Understanding the fundamental mechanisms governing the glass transition is crucial for designing materials with specific mechanical, thermal, and optical properties. The concept of a "solidity length" provides a concrete length scale that differentiates the behavior of ultra-viscous liquids from ordinary ones. While the paper acknowledges inconsistencies and areas needing further research, it provides a powerful lens through which to interpret experimental data and guide future theoretical and computational investigations into the enigmatic nature of glasses.
