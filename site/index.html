Quasi-phase-matched up- and down-conversion in periodically poled layered semiconductors

Chiara Trovatello, Carino Ferrante, Birui Yang, Josip Bajo, Benjamin Braun, Xinyi Xu et al.
The Brief

This research demonstrates a new way to convert light frequencies using ultra-thin, layered semiconductor materials.

The core innovation is using a specially structured material, a van der Waals semiconductor called 3R-MoS$_2$, that has been "periodically poled." This means its internal structure is patterned in a repeating way, which is crucial for "quasi-phase-matching." Quasi-phase-matching is a technique that ensures light waves of different frequencies interact efficiently, allowing for effective frequency conversion. This new material achieves significant frequency conversion efficiency with a thickness thousands of times smaller than previous technologies, and even shows enhanced performance due to its unique structure.

This breakthrough could lead to much smaller and more integrated devices for generating and manipulating light, which is fundamental to technologies like quantum computing and advanced sensors.

Background

Generating and manipulating light with specific properties is crucial for many technologies, from lasers to advanced computing. A key process in this is "nonlinear optics," where light interacts with a material to change its frequency or create new light properties, like entangled photons. For these processes to be efficient, a condition called "phase matching" must be met, which ensures the light waves stay in sync as they travel through the material. Traditionally, achieving this phase matching in efficient materials like lithium niobate requires very thick crystals, making them bulky and difficult to integrate into compact devices, especially for quantum technologies. This limitation hinders the miniaturization and widespread adoption of advanced light-based technologies.

What they did

This research tackles the challenge of achieving efficient light manipulation in ultra-thin materials. They focused on a specific type of layered semiconductor called 3R-molybdenum disulfide (3R-MoS$_2$). Unlike many other materials, 3R-MoS$_2$ has a strong "nonlinear optical response," meaning it's very good at interacting with light to change its properties. The key innovation here is "periodic poling," a technique borrowed from bulk crystals. In this context, it means creating a stack of 3R-MoS$_2$ layers where the orientation of the crystal structure is flipped every so often. This flipping is achieved by carefully stacking thin flakes of 3R-MoS$_2$ with a specific twist angle (180 degrees) between consecutive layers. Each layer is about the thickness of the "coherence length," which is the distance over which light waves can interact efficiently before going out of sync. By stacking these layers, they effectively create a "microstack" that acts like a single, specially engineered nonlinear optical material. This periodic structure allows them to achieve quasi-phase matching, a way to keep the light waves in sync even though the material itself isn't perfectly uniform. They then tested this material for two main nonlinear optical processes: second harmonic generation (SHG), where two lower-energy photons combine to create one higher-energy photon (like turning infrared light into visible light), and spontaneous parametric down-conversion (SPDC), where one high-energy photon splits into two lower-energy, entangled photons, crucial for quantum communication.

What they found

The researchers successfully created these periodically poled van der Waals semiconductors (PPTMDs) and demonstrated remarkable results. For second harmonic generation, they achieved a conversion efficiency of 0.03% at telecom wavelengths using a material only 3.4 micrometers thick, which is 10 to 100 times thinner than conventional systems with similar performance. This efficiency was even enhanced by about 50% beyond the expected theoretical gains due to internal "cavity effects" from light reflecting within the thin layers. For generating entangled photon pairs via SPDC, they achieved a high "coincidence-to-accidental-ratio" (CAR) of 638 ± 75, a key metric for the quality of photon sources. This CAR is significantly higher than what has been previously reported for microscopic sources made from van der Waals materials. They also showed that the phase-matching properties of their material could be tuned by adjusting the thickness of the individual layers and the number of stacked layers.

Why it matters

This work represents a significant leap forward in miniaturizing nonlinear optics and quantum light sources. By demonstrating efficient nonlinear optical processes in ultra-thin materials, it opens the door to integrating these functionalities onto a single chip. This is a critical step for building compact, on-chip quantum computers and advanced sensing devices, where bulky components are a major bottleneck. The ability to achieve macroscopic nonlinear effects with microscopic thicknesses means that complex optical systems can be drastically reduced in size, leading to more portable and scalable technologies. The use of van der Waals materials also offers advantages like flexibility and potential for integration with existing semiconductor manufacturing processes. While the current fabrication involves manual stacking, the underlying principles are scalable, paving the way for a new generation of ultra-compact and programmable photonic devices.
